Modelling

Our wound patch carries two molecules: the anti-inflammatory peptide V14 and the growth factor FGF2-G3. To understand how the patch behaves, we built a set of linked mathematical models that follow the drugs from the hydrogel into the wound tissue, onto their targets, and through to wound closure.

The aim of this work is not to build the most complex model possible, but one that is detailed enough to guide the design of the patch and the experiments of our wet lab.

Overview

The structure of the model

Our model is a chain of seven linked parts. It has one input, the amount of drug we load into the hydrogel, and two arms: a V14 arm (receptor binding, signalling and nitric oxide) and an FGF2-G3 arm (fibroblast growth and closure). The two arms meet at a single point, where the therapeutic state of the tissue sets the closure rate. Without that point this would be two unrelated models rather than a model of a combination patch.

The parts are not equally well supported, and the map below shows this. Models 1–2 can be used to make predictions now, because their parameters come from the V14 sequence, the patch geometry and the literature. Models 3–5 are used in scaled form, so they can run before any binding affinity is measured. Model 6 is a structured hypothesis: its coupling functions are phenomenological and no planned experiment constrains them yet. Marking this clearly shows where more data would help most.

__LEGEND__

The device

The patch is a single acellular calcium-alginate hydrogel layer, 500 µm thick: 1 % w/v sodium alginate, cross-linked with 100 mM calcium chloride, in HEPES-buffered saline at pH 7.4. Its outer face is sealed by an impermeable backing and its inner face sits on the wound bed. Two active molecules are mixed into the gel during casting: V14 (SVQELLELLAAGL, 1355.59 Da), variant 14 of our own de novo designs against the TLR4/MD2 complex, and FGF2-G3, an engineered, highly stable growth factor. The device contains no live bacteria.

01

Learning cycle

Why we removed the NO-sensing design

Summary

Nitric oxide (NO) would have fallen by at least two orders of magnitude before reaching even the thinnest sensor stack, so no promoter could have read the signal. The problem is geometric, not biological.

Our original design sensed wound nitric oxide with a NorR/PnorV promoter and responded by secreting the peptide, giving a closed-loop device that switches itself off. The promoter activity experiment did not work. Instead of treating this as a failed experiment only, we used the model to ask whether the design could have worked at all.

For NO diffusing through a porous layer while being consumed, the steady penetration depth is Ld = √(Deff / kdecay). Using a half-life of 0.09–2 s and sweeping the tortuosity group over its plausible range, we obtain Ld = 11–93 µm. The bacterial-cellulose and hydrogel stack of the earlier design was 0.5–3 mm thick.

We therefore removed the sensing branch. We kept its equations rather than deleting them: the two-layer transport operator was reused for Model 1, and the expression and secretion kinetics became an upstream production model for recombinant V14 and FGF2-G3. The resulting design is simpler, and every part of it can be tested.

02

Models 1 and 2

Release from the hydrogel and transport into tissue

Summary

The patch releases its load as a single burst rather than a sustained supply. From the 500 µm alginate gel as built, V14 reaches 95 % release in 11 minutes and FGF2-G3 in 25 minutes, while wound closure takes 7–21 days. The 2.4× difference between the two molecules comes only from their size, because plain alginate binds neither of them.

Three points follow from this. First, the gel diffusivity cannot be identified from closure data and has to be fitted from the release assay. Second, the model predicts only a small closure benefit from an unmodified gel. This is a prediction of the model, not a failure of it. Third, if release kinetics are to matter for efficacy, retention would have to be added to the gel. The next section asks what this would achieve. Our gel is plain alginate, which binds neither molecule: FGF-2 showed no measurable binding to unmodified alginate (Freeman et al., 2008), and V14 has no hydrophobic surface to stick to. That analysis is therefore a counterfactual.

The transport equations, and why porosity cancels

All concentrations in the model are interstitial, meaning mol per cm³ of pore fluid. For a porous medium with superficial flux N = −εDp∂xC, conservation gives ∂t(εC) = ∂x(εDp∂xC). For uniform porosity ε, it cancels and does not appear inside the PDE. Our earlier version applied it twice, once in the diffusivity and once in the concentration basis, which under-predicts spreading.

D_eff,i = D_0,i · H(a_i, ξ) / τ     H = exp[ −π ( (a_i + a_f) / (ξ + 2a_f) )² ]     τ = ε^(−1/2) (Bruggeman closure, replaced by a fitted τ at the first opportunity)

Porosity enters in only two places: the flux matching at the interface, and the conversion of loaded mass to concentration. At ξ = 14 nm, the mesh size measured for 1 % calcium alginate (Grassi et al., 2009), this gives Deff = 4.37×10−6 (V14) and 1.85×10−6 cm²s−1 (FGF2-G3), using the register's Stokes–Einstein diffusivities, hydrodynamic radii of 0.73 and 1.68 nm, and an alginate porosity of 0.99. The gel barely slows either molecule.

gel: ∂Cᵇ/∂t = D_effᵇ ∂²Cᵇ/∂x² − k_degᵇ Cᵇ 0 < x < L_gel tissue: ∂Cᵗ/∂t = D_effᵗ ∂²Cᵗ/∂x² − k_prot Cᵗ −L_tis < x < 0 ∂Cᵇ/∂x|_Lgel = 0 impermeable backing Cᵇ(0,t) = K_p Cᵗ(0,t) partition at the interface εᵇ Dᵇ ∂Cᵇ/∂x|_0⁺ = εᵗ Dᵗ ∂Cᵗ/∂x|_0⁻ flux matching, per total area Cᵗ(−L_tis, t) = 0 systemic sink at the dermal base

These four spatial conditions close the two coupled second-order domains, which we solve together as one system. Clearance to the circulation is imposed only at the deep boundary, so neither tissue equation carries a distributed clearance term: the two would describe the same physics. Our earlier version imposed both, which suppressed the tissue concentration twice. The only volumetric losses in the tissue are proteolysis, plus thermal denaturation and receptor uptake for FGF2-G3.

Solver verification

Before using any result, we checked the solvers against known answers. The release solver converges at second order against a closed-form benchmark, and the numerical Fisher–KPP front speed matches the analytic value to within 1.8–6.2 %.

03

Models 6 and 7

Wound closure and what it means for dosing

Summary

The gel as built is plain alginate (RV = 1, locked). One patch keeps tissue above the 25 µM target for 7.2 h, or 3.5–11.9 h across the ranges of sink depth and diffusivity. As a counterfactual, we also asked what retention would do. Retention works by binding, so the free concentration that drives delivery is Ctotal/RV: residence time is gained and driving concentration is lost in equal proportion. The window grows to about 13 h near RV ≈ 7 and falls to zero beyond RV ≈ 21.

Two conclusions follow for the design. First, gel thickness and dermal drainage set the single-patch window. Moving from our earlier 150 µm gel to the 500 µm alginate patch nearly triples it (2.6 to 7.2 h). Reducing kprot thirty-fold lengthens it only to 8.4 h, and retention at its optimum to about 13 h; both are smaller than the 3.5–11.9 h spread from the tissue parameters. Second, neither heparin nor alginate-sulfate can help V14. Both are strongly anionic and V14 has a net charge of −2 with no Lys, Arg or His, so they repel each other. Retaining V14 in alginate would need covalent tethering, so the two arms would need different chemistry.

A treatment that is too short lets the wound reopen

When the drug effect fades and reff becomes negative again, the steady state behind the front, nss = K(1 − d/rp), falls to zero. The repopulated tissue then dies back and the front recedes past its starting position. This resembles the relapse seen in chronic wounds, and it is a prediction that experiments can test.

Combining the delivery models with the closure model gives an end-to-end result, from the loaded dose to the healing outcome. It takes the form of a device specification:

Repeat dosing is therefore part of the design, not a fallback for a patch that failed to release slowly. The model sets the change interval, and the interval depends on the patch, not on the wound: wound size only changes how long treatment lasts.

Why does a daily change just miss? The drug effect averaged over a cycle equals the fraction of time a patch keeps tissue above target. Above 2.3 % the wound stops getting worse. To reach the density we count as closed, the average must exceed 33 %, which means 7.9 h of cover in every 24 h. One patch gives 7.2 h, so a daily change reaches 90 % of what is needed: the wound improves and then holds just short of closure. A slightly thicker gel (about 600–650 µm) or a loading near 1 mM would close the gap.

FGF2-G3 is not the limit. Even without any retention, the 500 µm gel keeps it above its EC50 for 2–3 days, longer than the change interval. Adding alginate-sulfate to hold it for weeks would be wasted when the dressing comes off every day. V14 sets the schedule.

Models 3 to 5 · identifiability and uncertainty

Receptor binding, the NO readout and sensitivity analysis

Summary

The V14 arm can be run before any binding affinity is measured. In the scaled variables ψ = CV/KD,P and a = [L]/KD,L, fourteen parameters reduce to four, and half-maximal suppression occurs at ψ50 = 1 + a exactly, with no fitted constant. Measuring KD,P rescales the axis, so it calibrates the model but is not needed to run it.

__I3__

Griess assay readout

The Griess assay measures accumulated nitrite, not free NO, and the two differ by roughly three orders of magnitude. Fitting the model's instantaneous free-NO expression to a Griess curve would compare two different quantities, so we fit the accumulated nitrite instead.

Sensitivity analysis

To find which parameters matter most, we ran Morris screening followed by Sobol indices over the full joint prior. The results contradicted two of our own expectations, which was the most useful outcome of the analysis.

05

Iteration

How running the model changed the model

Running the code corrected several statements in our written equation reference and did not support two of our predictions. We record them here so that future teams can see how the model was refined.

  1. The planar closure estimate is a lower bound, not an upper bound. Curvature does accelerate the front, by 5–21 % relative to Cartesian — but the formula Rw/c neglects the time to form the travelling front, and that transient outweighs the curvature gain. Radial closure exceeds Rw/c by 4–15 % (found with the earlier parameter set; not recomputed for the register update).
  2. The far-field boundary condition fails exactly where the device works. A zero-flux deep boundary needs Ltis ≫ Λ, but Λ is 1.1 mm at k = 10−4 s−1 and 11 mm at 10−6, against a dermis of 1–2 mm. V3 therefore uses a Dirichlet systemic sink, with no distributed clearance term as well.
  3. The Jensen gap was quoted at 15 %; recomputed for this geometry it is 0.2–36 %, median 2.7 %. The inherited figure was neither typical nor an upper bound. The gap is small in most of the parameter space but reaches 36 % in the corner that matters most, and it is always in the optimistic direction (again from the earlier parameter set).
  4. Retention is not a lever worth pulling. We wrote it up as something to be maximised. Running the coupled solve showed it at most doubles the window (7.2 to about 13 h), still well short of a day, and RV ≥ 21 gives zero hours above target. For V14 in alginate it would also need new tethering chemistry.
  5. Changing the gel from GelMA to calcium alginate mattered less than its thickness. Alginate binds neither molecule and its 14 nm mesh barely slows them, so the gel material drops out of the sensitivity analysis. The 500 µm thickness is what moved the answer: it raises one patch's cover from 2.6 to 7.2 h and the longest change interval from 7.8 to 21.8 h.
  6. A sub-threshold pulse causes relapse, not stalling. We had assumed a transient crossing of the rp > d threshold would simply fail to finish. It reverses.

We also found one modelling error during solver verification, and we note it because it is easy to repeat. With retention, the loaded concentration has to be treated as the total (free + bound), so the free initial concentration is Ctotal/RV. Treating the loading as free puts RV times more drug into the model than the solubility limit allows, and over-predicts sustained release by orders of magnitude.

Model → wet lab

Recommendations for the wet lab

The model is meant to guide what the wet lab does next. The experiments below are listed in priority order, ranked by how much each one would improve the model.

  • □
    V14 stability in serum or with recombinant MMP-9 / neutrophil elastase, by HPLC. Fixes kprot,V, the highest-priority unmeasured parameter in the register (currently a serum half-life proxy of 6.1 h). One day of work.
  • □
    Saturation solubility of V14 in the casting buffer. Sets Ctotal,0, and with it how long each patch lasts: a loading near 1 mM would make a daily change work. Trivial, cheap, and currently unplanned.
  • □
    Swelling and dissolution of the alginate gel in simulated wound fluid. Calcium alginate absorbs exudate and slowly dissolves as sodium replaces calcium. The model assumes a fixed 500 µm reservoir; swelling would dilute it and shorten the change interval. Weighing gels over 24 h is enough.
  • □
    Run the release assay against a RAW 264.7 layer rather than a stirred bath. Every planned experiment probes one module in isolation, so a seam error — a wrong interface value, a wrong porosity convention at the flux match — would pass all of them and still break the integrated prediction. This one test spans Models 1, 3 and 5 at once.
  • □
    The same release assay for FGF2-G3. Every FGF gel parameter is otherwise unconstrained, and it tests the predicted 2.4× arrival separation directly.
  • □
    Scratch assay on fibroblasts ± FGF2-G3, ± mitomycin C, reporting front width. One assay that supplies an otherwise entirely unsupported module and breaks the Dn / rp degeneracy.
  • □
    Two or three LPS concentrations in the existing flow-cytometry panel. Without them KD,P is not recoverable — only KDapp. Also run a Schild analysis: a depressed maximum would invalidate the competitive mechanism the whole V14 arm assumes.
  • □
    Nos2 as one of the two pro-inflammatory qPCR markers. It is the only marker that parameterises an equation already in the model.
  • □
    Sample the release assay on a log grid from ~10 s. Release from the 500 µm gel is ~95 % complete within 11–25 minutes, and about a third of the V14 is out in the first minute; minute-scale sampling would miss most of the kinetics.
  • □
    Test a patch-change protocol, not a single patch. The model puts the longest change interval that still closes a wound at about 21.8 h, so a daily change falls 10 % short. A 600–650 µm gel or a higher loading is the lever that would close that gap.

One limitation. If FGF2-G3 is assayed on fibroblasts and V14 on RAW 264.7 cells, the two arms are validated in different cell systems and cannot be validated together in vitro. A co-culture or sequential conditioned-medium experiment would be needed to test the combined effect directly.

✓

Conclusion

What the models tell us

Across the models, one conclusion appears consistently. With the 500 µm alginate gel as built, the patch delivers its load within minutes and keeps tissue above the V14 target for about 7 hours, while closing a wound takes days to weeks. A single patch therefore cannot close a wound, and the patch has to be changed at least every 21.8 h for closure in our model, which in practice means twice a day. A slightly thicker gel or a higher loading would make a daily change enough.

Retention does not rescue this design. It at most doubles the window, and for V14 it would need new chemistry. FGF2-G3 already lasts 2–3 days without it, so V14 sets the schedule. The V14 arm can be used in scaled form before its affinity is measured, whereas the closure model remains a hypothesis built on Tier-4 parameters. The wet-lab experiments listed above are the ones that would most improve it.
