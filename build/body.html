Overview

Background and structure of the model chain

Biological background and modelling question

We built a screening model and seven linked models to follow the patch from peptide choice to wound closure. The V14 arm represents release, transport, receptor binding, signalling and nitric oxide. The FGF2-G3 arm represents release, transport and fibroblast growth. Model 6 brings the two arms together: the modelled treatment state changes the rate of wound closure.

Each model represents one part of the wound-healing process.

Why chronic wounds can remain inflamed

Acute wounds usually move through four stages: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. Chronic wounds, including diabetic foot ulcers, venous leg ulcers and pressure ulcers, can remain in the inflammatory stage. Several processes can keep that inflammation going:

  • The receptor keeps receiving inflammatory signals. Bacterial LPS and molecules released by damaged host cells can activate TLR4. TLR4 needs the accessory protein MD2 to bind LPS and bring two receptor molecules together. In human diabetic wounds, TLR4 expression and downstream signalling are elevated rather than short-lived (Dasu & Martin, 2014).
  • The signal activates inflammatory pathways. When TLR4 and MD2 come together, they recruit MyD88 and activate NF-κB. This increases inflammatory cytokines, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and matrix metalloproteinases.
  • Inflammation can damage the tissue needed for repair. Chronic wound fluid contains roughly seven times more total MMP-9 than surrounding tissue, mostly in its active form (Moor et al., 2009). It also contains neutrophil elastase (Ai et al., 2004; Yager & Nwomeh, 1999). These enzymes break down the temporary matrix that cells move across and can also break down growth factors (Yager et al., 1997).
  • Damage can add to the original signal. Damaged cells and matrix release more signals that can activate TLR4, helping the inflammatory cycle continue.

This motivates a patch with two payloads. FGF2-G3 is intended to support fibroblast growth, while V14 is intended to reduce inflammatory signalling. Delivering a growth factor on its own may be less effective in a wound environment where proteases can break it down, as reported for exogenous growth factors in chronic wounds (Yager et al., 1997). Model 6 combines the two modelled effects when it estimates wound closure.

The device

The patch is a 2.5 mm layer of calcium-alginate hydrogel. It contains 1 % w/v sodium alginate cross-linked with 100 mM calcium chloride, in HEPES-buffered saline at pH 7.4. An impermeable backing seals the outer face, while the inner face contacts the wound bed. V14 (SVQELLELLAAGL, 1355.59 Da) and FGF2-G3 are mixed into the gel during casting. V14 is variant 14 of our de novo peptide designs against the TLR4/MD2 complex. FGF2-G3 is an engineered growth factor designed for greater stability. The patch contains no live bacteria.

V14 and αTI-14 are two names for the same molecule: SVQELLELLAAGL. The peptide-screening section uses αTI-14; the device models use V14. This page uses V14 throughout and gives the other name when it is first mentioned in each section.

Peptide suitability

Choosing a payload that survives the wound and the patch

Module 1: Peptide Suitability

1. Background

In synthetic biology, selecting a payload is often treated as a binding problem: find the sequence with the highest affinity and proceed. For a therapeutic peptide delivered via a hydrogel patch to a chronic wound, this is insufficient. The wound bed is a highly proteolytic environment, and the patch itself imposes structural constraints (Yager et al., 1997; Moor et al., 2009). A candidate that ignores these developability factors will fail, regardless of its theoretical target affinity. We observed this first-hand: our initial candidate, TAP2, failed to produce positive results in our wet-lab inflammation assays.

To investigate possible reasons for TAP2 failure and guide subsequent payload selection, we developed a mathematical screening model across three modules: binding suitability, structural burden, and proteolytic stability. The screen flags sequence-level liabilities in TAP2 and supports the selection of our de novo peptide, αTI-14. These liabilities are hypotheses about failure mechanisms; the inflammation assays do not establish which mechanism caused the observed outcome.

2. Model Construction

2.1 Assumptions

AssumptionJustification
The serum stability model predicts wound stability.No validated wound-fluid model exists; the Cavaco regression is the only fully specified sequence-based half-life model (129 peptides; Cavaco et al., 2021). Wound fluid is more proteolytic than serum, with elevated elastase and MMP-9 and depleted protease inhibitors (Yager et al., 1997; Moor et al., 2009), so the serum half-life is treated as an upper bound. At the current 2.5 mm thickness, the pure-diffusion release half-time is about 59 min, so the predicted V14 serum half-life of 6.1 h is about 6.2 times longer. This ratio describes the screening calculation at a fixed release timescale; it does not validate wound-fluid stability. In-gel degradation changes both the amount delivered and its timing, so a degradation-inclusive margin must be recalculated for the measured loss rate.
Structural burden is captured by Cys and Trp counts.Cys drives oxidative disulfide shuffling, and Trp drives hydrophobic aggregation at high loading concentrations (Shi & McHugh, 2023). These represent the two most predictable sequence-level failure modes for a hydrogel payload.
The 100 µM benchmark is transferable.MD2-I blocks LPS binding at 0.1 mM, representing the published state of the art for TLR4/MD2 peptides (Slivka et al., 2009). While assay conditions vary, this provides a necessary reference point for judging our candidate's potency.

2.2 Governing Equations

The model evaluates three independent modules, each returning a sub-score s_i \in [0,1]. Because these represent independent ways a payload can fail, we combine them using a geometric mean. This ensures that a near-zero score in any single module (e.g., severe aggregation risk) drags the overall score down, rather than being averaged out by high scores elsewhere.

S = \left( \prod_{i \in \{A,B,C\}} s_i \right)^{1/3}

Where: S is the composite suitability score; s_A, s_B, s_C are the sub-scores for binding suitability, structural burden, and stability, respectively.

The verdict is not based on the composite score alone, but is gated by the two sequence-level modules:

\text{Verdict} = \begin{cases} \text{suitable}, & n_{\text{Cys}} \leq 1 \text{ and } s_C \geq \frac{1}{2} \\ \text{unsuitable}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

Where: n_{\text{Cys}} \leq 1 is the implemented structural gate: fewer than two cysteine residues prevents an intramolecular disulfide pair. The stability gate s_C \geq 1/2 requires the predicted degradation half-life to be at least the release half-time. The combined structural score s_B reports both Cys and Trp burdens, but is not itself a pass/fail threshold.

Module A: Binding Suitability

We assume the payload competes with LPS for the MD2 pocket and model that hypothesis as a competitive binding equilibrium. The fractions of MD2 occupied by the inhibitor (\phi_{\text{inhib}}) and by LPS (\phi_{\text{LPS}}) are:

\phi_{\text{inhib}} = \frac{[P]/K_{D,P}}{1 + [L]/K_{D,L} + [P]/K_{D,P}}, \quad \phi_{\text{LPS}} = \frac{[L]/K_{D,L}}{1 + [L]/K_{D,L} + [P]/K_{D,P}}

Where: [P] is the peptide concentration; [L] is the LPS concentration; K_{D,P} is the peptide-MD2 dissociation constant; K_{D,L} is the LPS-MD2 dissociation constant.

The predicted blockade fraction \chi_{\text{block}} is the ratio of inhibitor-bound to total bound receptor:

\chi_{\text{block}} = \frac{\phi_{\text{inhib}}}{\phi_{\text{inhib}} + \phi_{\text{LPS}}}

Where: \chi_{\text{block}} ranges from 0 (no receptor blockade) to 1 (complete blockade).

We convert this to a sub-score by comparing the candidate's half-maximal inhibitory concentration (C_{50}^{\text{cand}}) to the literature benchmark of 100 µM (Slivka et al., 2009):

s_A = \min\left(1, \frac{C_{\text{bench}}}{C_{50}^{\text{cand}}}\right)

Where: s_A is the binding sub-score, capped at 1; C_{\text{bench}} = 100\ \mu\text{M}, the blocking concentration of the MD2-I benchmark peptide; and C_{50}^{\text{cand}} is the half-maximal inhibitory concentration of the candidate relative to LPS-only signal. A single tested dose is not a measured half-maximal concentration. For V14, any numerical score based on the current affinity proxy is conditional on the unvalidated gate-to-occupancy assumption.

Module B: Structural Burden

Two residue classes carry developability risk. Cysteine forms disulfides, creating folding heterogeneity. We apply a progressive penalty:

s_{B,\text{Cys}} = 1 - \frac{n_{\text{Cys}}}{n_{\text{Cys}} + 1}

Where: s_{B,\text{Cys}} drops from 1 (zero Cys) to 0.50 (one Cys) to 0.33 (two Cys); n_{\text{Cys}} is the number of cysteine residues.

Tryptophan drives aggregation. We apply a monotonic exponential penalty:

s_{B,\text{Trp}} = \exp(-0.5 \cdot n_{\text{Trp}})

Where: s_{B,\text{Trp}} is the aggregation penalty; n_{\text{Trp}} is the number of tryptophan residues; the decay rate 0.5 is a design choice, as there is no quantitative aggregation model in the literature.

The structural score is their product:

s_B = s_{B,\text{Cys}} \cdot s_{B,\text{Trp}}

Where: s_B is the combined structural burden sub-score.

Module C: Proteolytic Stability

We estimate the serum half-life t_{1/2}^{\text{serum}} using the Cavaco regression (Cavaco et al., 2021) and compare it to the release half-time t_{1/2,\text{rel}} of the hydrogel patch. The stability score is:

s_C = \frac{t_{1/2}^{\text{deg}}}{t_{1/2}^{\text{deg}} + t_{1/2,\text{rel}}}

Where: s_C is the stability sub-score; t_{1/2}^{\text{deg}} \approx t_{1/2}^{\text{serum}}, the predicted degradation half-life; and t_{1/2,\text{rel}} is the release half-time of the patch (calculated as 0.196 L^2 / D_{\text{eff}} from the companion transport model).

3. Simulated Results

At the current 2.5 mm thickness, the sequence gates retain MD2-II and V14. TAP1 fails the stability gate; TAP2 and MD2-I fail both gates; TAP3 fails the cysteine gate despite passing the stability gate. V14 has the longest predicted serum half-life, 6.1 h, with s_B=1 and s_C\approx0.86. MD2-II passes stability only marginally (s_C\approx0.51) and is an inactive binding control: passing the sequence gates does not establish binding activity.

Using the conditional V14 binding score of 1 described below gives a composite score S\approx0.95. This is a screening score dependent on the occupancy assumption and predicted serum stability, not a measured probability of success.

4. Experimental evidence and validation limits

We compared the screening predictions with flow cytometry and RT-qPCR observations, while separating the measured responses from the proposed mechanisms.

Module A: single-dose flow evidence. In the 17 October panel on PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages, 25 µM αTI-14 produced a 21.34% decrease in positive-gate frequency relative to FITC-LPS alone (p = 0.0186, n = 4 per group). This supports reduced LPS-associated fluorescence. The gate-to-occupancy mapping is unvalidated, so this result does not establish a binding affinity, a measured IC50, or the competitive mechanism. Equating the gate-frequency ratio with relative LPS occupancy gives the exploratory KD,P proxy of 5.62 µM and a model-based relative half-inhibition dose of about 92 µM at 1 µM LPS. That conditional dose gives s_A=1 against the 100 µM benchmark; the score is a model result, not an experimentally validated potency.

Modules B and C: sequence-based hypotheses. The screen rejects TAP2 because it has two cysteines and its predicted serum half-life is shorter than the current gel release half-time. Its three tryptophans also lower the structural score. RT-qPCR showed that TAP2 did not suppress LPS-induced inflammatory markers in the tested M0 and M1 conditions. This outcome is consistent with an unsuitable payload, but does not identify disulfide formation, aggregation or degradation as the cause. Direct structural and stability measurements are needed to test those explanations.

Where: Flow cytometry measures positive-gate frequency in this panel; it does not directly measure receptor occupancy. RT-qPCR measures downstream transcript responses and cannot by itself validate the structural or stability mechanisms. M0 and M1 refer to the uncommitted and pro-inflammatory macrophage conditions, assessed at 6 h and 24 h respectively.

5. Sensitivity Analysis

Because this is a discrete sequence-screening model, sensitivity analysis evaluates whether the final verdicts are robust to the model's two primary design choices, rather than sweeping continuous physical parameters.

Trp decay constant (\lambda_{\text{Trp}}). The decay rate of 0.5 is an assumed scoring parameter. Changing it alters the structural and composite scores for Trp-containing peptides, but cannot change the implemented gate verdicts: those depend only on cysteine count and the stability score. This invariance follows from the gate definition and does not validate the Trp penalty.

Release half-time (t_{1/2,\text{rel}}). The release half-time is governed by the gel thickness, calculated via the companion transport model. While the stability gate becomes more stringent at thicker geometries (failing TAP1 and MD2-I at the 2.5 mm design), αTI-14 maintains a passing verdict across the entire biologically plausible thickness range (0.5 mm to 4 mm).

Binding benchmark (C_{\text{bench}}). With the conditional V14 half-inhibition dose of about 92 µM, a benchmark of 50 µM gives s_A\approx0.54, while benchmarks of 100 or 200 µM give the capped score of 1. The binding score therefore depends on the chosen benchmark and the unvalidated occupancy assumption. The two sequence-gate verdicts do not depend on this score.

Module 1 · Co-diffusion (Models 1 and 2)

Release from the hydrogel and transport into tissue

1. Background

The transport of therapeutic molecules from a polymeric reservoir into biological tissue is governed by reaction–diffusion dynamics. In our device, the two payloads (αTI-14, our peptide variant V14, and FGF2-G3) with distinct physicochemical properties must transition from the highly hydrated alginate matrix into the proteolytically active wound bed.

The model represents this system as two coupled spatial domains: a gel domain (0 ≤ x ≤ Lgel) and a tissue domain (−Ltis ≤ x ≤ 0). We solve the Fickian diffusion equation in both domains, using an effective diffusivity that accounts for the steric hindrance and tortuosity of the polymer network. In the tissue domain, we include first-order proteolytic degradation, representing the hostile enzymatic environment of a chronic wound. For FGF2-G3, we add an additional Michaelis–Menten sink to represent saturable receptor-mediated uptake by fibroblasts.

The two domains are solved monolithically to enforce mass conservation and thermodynamic partitioning across the interface. The model outputs the interstitial concentration profile of each payload as a function of time and depth, which determines the duration for which the target cells in the tissue remain above the therapeutic threshold.

2. Model Construction

2.1 Assumptions

AssumptionJustification
Transport is one-dimensional.The patch is a wide, thin slab; over the region feeding a millimetre-scale wound, lateral gradients are negligible compared to the vertical axis (Crank, 1975).
The gel is a homogeneous, constant reservoir.Gel properties are modelled as constant over the release window. Physical swelling and dissolution occur over hours to days, which can be mitigated by reapplication of the patch.
Degradation follows pseudo-first-order kinetics.At therapeutic concentrations far below the enzyme half-saturation constant (K_M), proteolysis simplifies to first-order loss. Chronic wound fluid is highly proteolytic due to elevated elastase and diminished inhibitors (Yager et al., 1997).
Only FGF2-G3 undergoes saturable receptor-mediated uptake.FGF2-G3 binds and is internalised by fibroblast receptors via a Michaelis–Menten mechanism. The model treats αTI-14 as having no equivalent receptor sink, as its target (MD2) is present at nanomolar levels that cannot deplete a micromolar payload.
Boundary conditions: sealed backing and systemic sink.The outer face is an impermeable backing. The deep tissue boundary is a fixed-concentration sink because the dermis is vascularised and drains solutes systemically (Braverman, 1997).
Neutral thermodynamic partition at the interface (K_p = 1).Both phases are >95% water, so the mobile pool of each payload partitions equally across the interface. Unmodified alginate is not expected to bind either payload strongly: FGF-2 binds sulfated but not unmodified alginate (Freeman et al., 2008), and αTI-14 (net charge −2) is electrostatically repelled. The part of the load that the release assay suggests is held in the gel is carried separately by the mobile fraction (Section 4.2), not by K_p.

2.2 Governing Equations

The transport of each payload is governed by Fick's second law, modified to account for degradation and, in the tissue, receptor-mediated uptake. Because the gel and tissue are distinct materials, we solve them as two coupled spatial domains: the gel (0 \le x \le L_{\mathrm{gel}}) and the tissue (-L_{\mathrm{tis}} \le x \le 0).

Gel Release (Model 1). Inside the hydrogel, the payload diffuses and undergoes first-order degradation. The concentration C^{\mathrm g}_i(x,t) for species i (where i \in \{V, F\} for αTI-14 and FGF2-G3) is given by:

(1) Gel release
\frac{\partial C^{\mathrm g}_i}{\partial t}=D^{\mathrm g}_{\mathrm{eff},i}\frac{\partial^2 C^{\mathrm g}_i}{\partial x^2}-k^{\mathrm g}_{\mathrm{deg},i}\,C^{\mathrm g}_i

Where: C^{\mathrm g}_i(x,t) is the concentration in the gel (mol cm⁻³); D^{\mathrm g}_{\mathrm{eff},i} is the effective diffusivity in the gel, calculated via Amsden obstruction scaling (Eq. 4; Amsden, 1998); k^{\mathrm g}_{\mathrm{deg},i} is the first-order degradation rate in the gel (s⁻¹).

Tissue Transport (Model 2). Once the payload crosses the interface into the tissue, it is subjected to proteolytic degradation. For FGF2-G3, we add a Michaelis–Menten term to account for saturable receptor-mediated uptake by fibroblasts. The concentration in the tissue, C^{\mathrm t}_i(x,t), is:

(2) αTI-14 in tissue
\frac{\partial C^{\mathrm t}_V}{\partial t}=D^{\mathrm t}_{\mathrm{eff},V}\frac{\partial^2 C^{\mathrm t}_V}{\partial x^2}-k_{\mathrm{prot},V}\,C^{\mathrm t}_V
(3) FGF2-G3 in tissue
\frac{\partial C^{\mathrm t}_F}{\partial t}=D^{\mathrm t}_{\mathrm{eff},F}\frac{\partial^2 C^{\mathrm t}_F}{\partial x^2}-\bigl(k_{\mathrm{deg},F}+k_{\mathrm{prot},F}\bigr)C^{\mathrm t}_F-\frac{V_{\max}\,C^{\mathrm t}_F}{K_M+C^{\mathrm t}_F}\,\rho_{\mathrm{fib}}

Where: C^{\mathrm t}_i(x,t) is the concentration in the tissue (mol cm⁻³); D^{\mathrm t}_{\mathrm{eff},i} is the effective diffusivity in the tissue (cm² s⁻¹); k_{\mathrm{prot},i} is the tissue proteolysis rate (s⁻¹); V_{\max} and K_M are the FGFR internalisation capacity (mol cell⁻¹ s⁻¹) and half-saturation constant (mol cm⁻³); \rho_{\mathrm{fib}} is the local fibroblast density (cells cm⁻³).

Effective Diffusivity. The diffusion coefficient inside the gel is not the free-water value; it is reduced by the polymer network. We calculate this using the Amsden obstruction scaling model (Amsden, 1998):

(4) Amsden obstruction scaling
D^{\mathrm g}_{\mathrm{eff},i}=D_{0,i}\,\frac{H(a_i,\xi)}{\tau}

Where: D_{0,i} is the free-solution diffusivity at 37 °C; H(a_i,\xi) is the steric hindrance factor, a function of the solute hydrodynamic radius a_i and the alginate mesh size \xi; \tau is the tortuosity.

Boundary and Initial Conditions. The two domains are solved monolithically, meaning they are joined by interface conditions rather than solved separately. Four spatial boundary conditions and two initial conditions are required to close the system:

(5) Initial condition (uniform loading)
C^{\mathrm g}_i(x,0)=f_{\mathrm{mob},i}\,C_{i,0},\qquad C^{\mathrm t}_i(x,0)=0

Where: C_{i,0} is the initial loaded concentration in the gel; f_{\mathrm{mob},i} is the mobile, release-competent fraction of the load (Section 4.2). The tissue is assumed to be payload-free at t = 0.

(6) Impermeable backing (no flux at x = Lgel)
\left.\frac{\partial C^{\mathrm g}_i}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L_{\mathrm{gel}}}=0

Where: the outer face of the patch is sealed, so no payload can escape upward.

(7) Thermodynamic partition (at x = 0)
C^{\mathrm g}_i(0,t)=K_{p,i}\,C^{\mathrm t}_i(0,t)

Where: K_{p,i} is the partition coefficient, set to 1 because both phases are >95% water and the mobile pool is not expected to bind unmodified alginate (Freeman et al., 2008). Any retained pool enters through f_{\mathrm{mob},i}, not through K_{p,i}.

(8) Flux matching (at x = 0)
\varepsilon_{\mathrm g}D^{\mathrm g}_{\mathrm{eff},i}\left.\frac{\partial C^{\mathrm g}_i}{\partial x}\right|_{0^+}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm t}D^{\mathrm t}_{\mathrm{eff},i}\left.\frac{\partial C^{\mathrm t}_i}{\partial x}\right|_{0^-}

Where: \varepsilon_{\mathrm g} and \varepsilon_{\mathrm t} are the porosities of the gel and tissue. This condition ensures that mass is conserved across the interface: whatever leaves the gel enters the tissue.

(9) Systemic sink (at x = −Ltis)
C^{\mathrm t}_i(-L_{\mathrm{tis}},t)=0

Where: the deep dermis is vascularised and drains solutes systemically, so the concentration is clamped to zero at the base of the tissue domain (Braverman, 1997).

3. Solver Verification

Before using any result, we checked the solvers against known answers. Verification of this kind establishes that the solvers reproduce known mathematics; it is not validation against experiment, and we do not present it as such.

Release solver against the Crank series. For the special case of a perfect sink with first-order loss, the gel problem admits a closed-form solution, the Crank series (Crank, 1975):

Crank series · the closed-form benchmark
C(x,t)=C_0e^{-kt}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{4}{(2m+1)\pi}\sin\!\left[\frac{(2m+1)\pi x}{2L}\right]\exp\!\left[-\frac{D(2m+1)^2\pi^2t}{4L^2}\right]

whose cumulative release,

Cumulative release
\frac{M_t}{M_\infty}=1-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{8}{(2m+1)^2\pi^2}\exp\!\left[-\frac{D(2m+1)^2\pi^2t}{4L^2}\right]

is the benchmark the release solver is verified against. Run for αTI-14 in a 500 µm gel, the solver converges at second order in space: the maximum deviation from the closed form falls from 5.2×10⁻⁵ at 100 grid points to 2.0×10⁻⁷ at 1600, a factor of exactly 4 per grid doubling. Mass is conserved: the mass retained in the gel matches the analytic cumulative release to 1.0×10⁻⁷ at every output time. Because the loaded gel meets a perfect sink at t = 0, the release flux is singular at the interface, so release must be integrated from the retained mass (or sampled on a log time grid), not from the flux on a uniform grid. The solver also reproduces the physical limits: release stalls as diffusivity vanishes, and the sequestered (immobile) pool never releases.

Fisher–KPP front speed. The numerical front speed of the closure model (Model 6) matches the analytic value (Fisher, 1937; Kolmogorov et al., 1937) to within 1.8–6.2%, sitting just below the analytic line, the expected behaviour for a discretised pulled front.

4. Experimental Comparison & Model Refinement

4.1 The first release assay: FGF2-G3

The model's first quantitative test was a BCA release assay of FGF2-G3 from the cast alginate gel.

The assay tested the model's baseline prediction: with neutral partitioning at the gel–tissue interface (K_p = 1) and a fully mobile load, Model 1 predicts essentially complete release within the first day (FGF2-G3 release half-time ≈ 2.6 h; 95% by ≈ 15 h for the 2.5 mm design), and 31–44% of the loaded dose released between 60 and 120 min.

The measured release was lower than this prediction. The blank-subtracted, positive-control-normalised means were 13.3%, 13.8% and 22.9% of the loaded dose at 60, 90 and 120 min (three wells each). Fitting the model release curve, scaled by a mobile fraction, to all nine wells gives f_{\mathrm{mob},F} = 0.447 (residual standard error 0.055). The assay therefore does not support a fully mobile FGF2-G3 load, but it cannot yet show a plateau: it has three time points, all within the first two hours, the signals sit near the BCA noise floor, and the gel thickness and buffer volume were not recorded. We treat the result as a provisional estimate, not as a validation of the release model. αTI-14 was not measured (Section 4.2).

4.2 The retention split

The model was refined to carry retention explicitly. The loaded dose of each payload is now split into two pools at the initial condition:

Mobile pool at t = 0
C^{\mathrm g}_i(x,0)=f_{\mathrm{mob},i}\,C_{i,0}

where f_{\mathrm{mob},i} is the mobile, release-competent fraction, and the complement (1 - f_{\mathrm{mob},i}) is a sequestered pool with zero diffusivity that degrades in place and never releases. The entire release curve is rescaled:

Rescaled release curve
f_{\mathrm{rel},i}(t)=f_{\mathrm{mob},i}\,f_{\mathrm{slab},i}(t)

where f_{\mathrm{slab},i}(t) is the closed-form solution, so the curve plateaus at f_{\mathrm{mob},i} rather than at 1. The sequestered pool is carried in the mass balance, so the balance still closes. The new parameters:

An HPLC release assay is needed to fix this value.

Why part of the load may be retained. Steric trapping is unlikely at this scale: both payloads are small compared with a ~14 nm mesh (hydrodynamic radius 0.87 and 2.34 nm). If the retention holds up in a properly sampled assay, it would point to specific binding. Candidate mechanisms, none tested yet, are Ca²⁺-bridging between alginate carboxylates and acidic side chains, and hydrophobic association with the polymer backbone (αTI-14 is 69% nonpolar by composition; it was designed to enter MD2's hydrophobic pocket). Freeman et al. (2008) found that FGF-2 does not bind unmodified alginate; if the FGF2-G3 retention is confirmed, that result would not carry over to this variant in this gel. This is the model–assay loop working as intended: the dry lab assumed full mobility, the first wet-lab measurement did not fit it, and the model now carries retention as an explicit, provisional parameter until a log-grid release assay confirms or removes it.

The refinement is also the clearest example of the two-way coupling between the models and the experiments in this project: f_{\mathrm{mob}} entered Models 1–2 as a fitted quantity rather than an assumed one (provisional for FGF2-G3, assumed for αTI-14), and every downstream number (delivered concentrations, loading requirements, closure predictions) inherits it.

5. Simulated Results

Release is staggered by size, and both curves plateau at retention. From the 2.5 mm gel as designed, αTI-14 releases with a half-time of 59 min and is 95% released by 5.6 h; FGF2-G3, the larger molecule, releases with a half-time of ≈ 2.6 h and is 95% of its mobile pool released by ≈ 15 h. The difference between the two molecules comes only from their size, and it schedules the two payloads without any design effort: the anti-inflammatory peptide delivers as a pulse over the first hours after dressing change, while the growth factor's release spans the wear interval as a sustained supply. About half of each load is held in the gel as a sequestered pool (fitted for FGF2-G3, assumed for αTI-14).

The timescale gap makes the chain modular. Wound closure takes 7–21 days; release completes in 5.6 h (αTI-14) to ~15 h (FGF2-G3), a 30–90× and 11–34× separation respectively. This separation is what lets the models form a chain at all: because release is far faster than closure, the release problem and the healing problem decouple, and Model 1's output can drive Models 3–6 as a boundary condition instead of one coupled moving-boundary problem.

Module 3 · Receptor occupancy (Model 3)

Competition for MD2/TLR4

1. Background

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from bacteria in the wound bed activates macrophages by binding the hydrophobic pocket of MD2, which then dimerises TLR4 and starts NF-κB signalling. Our peptide αTI-14 (V14) was designed to occupy the same pocket. If it does, it competes with LPS for MD2, and the fraction of receptors that LPS can occupy falls as the V14 concentration in the tissue rises.

The model represents this system as a single binding equilibrium. V14 and LPS compete for one pool of MD2, each forming a complex with its own dissociation constant, and the concentration of V14 delivered to the tissue by Model 2 sets how much of the pool is blocked. We solve the equilibrium in closed form and rescale it so that the predictions do not depend on the V14 affinity, which has not been measured.

The model outputs the fraction of MD2 occupied by LPS as a function of the V14 concentration in the tissue. This is the input to the NF-κB signalling model (Model 4), and it sets the V14 concentration that the patch must hold in the tissue, which is the design target for Model 7.

2.2 Governing Equations

V14 (P) and LPS (L) compete for the same pocket of MD2, forming an inactive complex MD2·P or an active pro-inflammatory complex MD2·L. We write the mass balance on the receptor, apply mass action at equilibrium, and then rescale.

Mass balance and equilibria. The total MD2 is shared between the free receptor and the two complexes:

(1) Receptor mass balance
[\mathrm{MD2}]_{\mathrm{tot}}=[\mathrm{MD2}]_{\mathrm{free}}+[\mathrm{MD2}\!\cdot\!P]+[\mathrm{MD2}\!\cdot\!L]

At rapid local equilibrium, with K_{\mathrm{D},P}=k_{\mathrm{off},P}/k_{\mathrm{on},P} and K_{\mathrm{D},L}=k_{\mathrm{off},L}/k_{\mathrm{on},L},

(2) Equilibria
[\mathrm{MD2}\!\cdot\!P]=\frac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}}\,[\mathrm{MD2}]_{\mathrm{free}},\qquad[\mathrm{MD2}\!\cdot\!L]=\frac{[L]}{K_{\mathrm{D},L}}\,[\mathrm{MD2}]_{\mathrm{free}}

Where: C^{\mathrm t}_V is the interstitial V14 concentration in the tissue (the output of Model 2); [L] is the LPS concentration; K_{\mathrm{D},P} and K_{\mathrm{D},L} are the dissociation constants of the V14–MD2 and LPS–MD2 complexes.

Fractional occupancies. Substituting Eq. (2) into Eq. (1) and eliminating the free receptor gives the fraction of MD2 held by each ligand. Each occupancy carries the other ligand's concentration in its denominator:

(3) Competitive occupancy at MD2
\theta_{\mathrm{inhib}}=\frac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}\!\left(1+\dfrac{[L]}{K_{\mathrm{D},L}}\right)+C^{\mathrm t}_V}=\frac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm D}^{\mathrm{app}}+C^{\mathrm t}_V},\qquad\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}=\frac{[L]/K_{\mathrm{D},L}}{1+\dfrac{[L]}{K_{\mathrm{D},L}}+\dfrac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}}}

Where: \theta_{\mathrm{inhib}} and \theta_{\mathrm{LPS}} are the fractions of the MD2 pool occupied by V14 and by LPS, and K_{\mathrm D}^{\mathrm{app}}=K_{\mathrm{D},P}(1+[L]/K_{\mathrm{D},L}) is the apparent dissociation constant of V14 in the presence of LPS.

Scaled variables. Rescaling concentration by the two dissociation constants removes them from the problem. The whole competition collapses onto a single curve family indexed by the scaled endotoxin load a, which for our register value of 1 µM wound endotoxin is a = 15.4. Fourteen parameters reduce to four:

(4) Scaled variables
\psi=\frac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}},\qquad a=\frac{[L]}{K_{\mathrm{D},L}},\qquad\gamma=\frac{[\mathrm{TLR4}]_{\mathrm{tot}}}{K_{\mathrm{NF}}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\theta_{\mathrm{inhib}}=\frac{\psi}{1+a+\psi},\quad\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}=\frac{a}{1+a+\psi}

Where: ψ is the dimensionless V14 concentration, a is the dimensionless endotoxin load, and γ is the scaled receptor level used by Model 4.

Half-maximal suppression. The suppression ratio then has no free constant left in it. Setting it to one half gives a half-maximal dose with no fitted parameter anywhere in it:

(5) Suppression ratio and half-maximal dose
\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}(\psi)}{\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}(0)}=\frac{1+a}{1+a+\psi}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\boxed{\;\psi_{50}=1+a\;}

Where: ψ50 is the scaled V14 dose that halves the LPS-bound fraction of MD2 relative to its untreated value. With a = 15.4, ψ50 = 16.4.

The design target is a different quantity. The tissue concentration that the patch must hold is set by absolute occupancy, not by the relative suppression in Eq. (5). Requiring half of the MD2 pool to be LPS-bound, \theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}=1/2, gives \psi=a-1:

(6) Design target
C_{V,\mathrm{target}}=K_{\mathrm{D},P}\,(a-1),\qquad\mathrm{IC}_{50}=K_{\mathrm D}^{\mathrm{app}}=K_{\mathrm{D},P}\,(1+a)

Where: C_{V,\mathrm{target}} is the tissue concentration at which half of MD2 is LPS-bound; IC50 is the dose at which the LPS-bound fraction is half of its untreated value, which is the half-maximal point that a flow-cytometry dose–response would report. The two are not interchangeable.

The assay readout. The flow-cytometry experiment reports a normalised fluorescence, not an occupancy. If the signal is the mean intensity (MFI), it is linear in the occupancy ratio:

(7) Readout and what it can identify
\mathrm{MFI}=\mathrm{MFI}_{\mathrm{bg}}+(\mathrm{MFI}_{\max}-\mathrm{MFI}_{\mathrm{bg}})\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}(C^{\mathrm t}_V)}{\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}(0)},\qquad\frac{\mathrm{IC}_{50}^{(v)}}{\mathrm{IC}_{50}^{(w)}}=\frac{K_{\mathrm{D},P}^{(v)}}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}^{(w)}}

Where: MFIbg and MFImax are the negative and positive controls, and (v), (w) index two V14 variants assayed at the same LPS concentration. The half-maximal point of this readout is K_{\mathrm D}^{\mathrm{app}}, not K_{\mathrm{D},P}: recovering K_{\mathrm{D},P} needs [L] and K_{\mathrm{D},L} (Cheng and Prusoff, 1973). Ratios between variants are identifiable at a single fixed [L], because the endotoxin factor cancels.

Our measured readout is a positive-gate frequency, not an MFI. A gate frequency is a tail probability of the per-cell intensity distribution and is not linear in occupancy, so its relation to Eq. (7) was not calibrated. Section 4.1 explains what we assumed and why.

4. Experimental Comparison & Model Refinement

4.1 The flow-cytometry assay

The model's first quantitative test was a flow-cytometry assay of FITC-labelled LPS binding to cells, with and without V14. We state precisely what was and was not measured. At 25 µM, V14 reduced the FITC-LPS positive-gate frequency from 16.307% to 12.827%, a 3.480-percentage-point decrease and a 21.34% relative reduction (p = 0.0186, n = 4 per group). This indicates reduced FITC-LPS positivity under the assay conditions. It does not establish direct competition at MD2.

Positive-gate frequency is a thresholded readout whose relationship to receptor occupancy was not calibrated, so these data do not determine a binding dissociation constant. For exploratory modelling only, we equate the gate-frequency ratio with the relative LPS occupancy ratio of Eq. (5). Solving Eq. (5) for K_{\mathrm{D},P} at the tested dose gives:

(8) Exploratory model-based estimate (assumes gate ratio equals relative LPS occupancy)
r_{\mathrm{gate}}=\frac{12.827}{16.307}=0.7866,\qquad K_{\mathrm{D},P}=\frac{25}{\left(1+\frac{1}{0.065}\right)\left(\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{gate}}}-1\right)}=5.62\,\mu\mathrm{M},\qquad C_{V,\mathrm{target}}=K_{\mathrm{D},P}(a-1)=80.9\,\mu\mathrm{M}

Where: r_{\mathrm{gate}} is the V14 to LPS-only positive-gate-frequency ratio; 25 µM is the tested V14 dose; and a = [L]/K_{\mathrm{D},L} = 1/0.065 = 15.4. The estimated K_{\mathrm{D},P} and the derived target depend on the assumption that the gate ratio equals the relative LPS occupancy. They are not experimentally determined quantities.

This is a single-dose proxy. An LPS titration and a calibrated, occupancy-linked readout (MFI or FCS histograms) are needed to identify an absolute affinity; positive-gate frequency alone does not provide it. Ratios between V14 variants are already identifiable at a single fixed [L], so the variant screen works as planned. We also ask for a Schild analysis in the same run, because a depressed maximum would invalidate the competitive mechanism that the whole V14 arm assumes. That is the one result that would force us to rebuild this model rather than refit it.

4.2 The scaling correction

To invert the model for the required dose, ψ has to be isolated additively. Our earlier version factored out K_{\mathrm D}^{\mathrm{app}}, which is valid for the occupancy expression and for the Cheng–Prusoff relation but not for this inversion. The resulting dose–load relation came out quadratic in the endotoxin load instead of linear, so the error was in the scaling law and not merely in a prefactor: the over-estimate grew from about 2× at low endotoxin load to about 2500× at high load. The earlier version mispredicted the very quantity it was built to deliver, and only a direct numerical check caught it. The model now carries the exact result of Eq. 2. Model Construction: signalling and integrated readout

Models 4 and 5 use Hill functions to represent the signalling response without a full reaction network. A detailed NF-κB model could include IKK activation, IκBα degradation and resynthesis, and p65 nuclear shuttling. Such a model would introduce more than a dozen additional parameters, which the planned experiments do not constrain. The current models instead use a monotonic, saturating input–output relationship between receptor occupancy and transcriptional drive. A Hill function is a compact form for this relationship, and its exponent can in principle be estimated from dose–response data.

This simplification does not represent signalling dynamics. NF-κB can oscillate, so a pulsed input may produce a different transcriptional response from a steady input with the same average. The steady-state response should therefore be interpreted as an approximation.

Model 5 distinguishes two calculated quantities. Free NO approaches a quasi-steady level on the modelled timescale, while accumulated nitrite integrates the predicted production rate. The model therefore reports both the instantaneous free-NO concentration and the integral for nitrite. The earlier version matched a free-NO expression to nitrite values from literature, hiding a 104× error in the per-cell iNOS rate; the current formulation keeps those quantities separate. These remain theoretical outputs, not a fit to team measurements.

A reduced sigmoid could describe a dose–response if such data existed, but no Griess experiment is planned. The internal constants are not calibrated, and no fitted IC50NO or napp is reported.

The chain from V14 concentration to nitrite contains at least fourteen parameters. No team nitrite dose–response data exist and no Griess assay is planned, so these parameters are not calibrated and no fit is reported. Model 5 remains an illustrative calculation.

The assumption we are least comfortable with

Model 6 reads nitrite as a monotonic index of harm: more nitrite means lower proliferation and higher death. The biology does not straightforwardly support that. Nitric oxide is required for normal repair — iNOS-deficient animals and animals given iNOS inhibitors show delayed re-epithelialisation and reduced collagen deposition and tensile strength, and NO has a central role in the angiogenesis that granulation tissue depends on (Krausz & Friedman, 2015). Macrophage-derived NO also contributes to antimicrobial defence in a wound that is, by assumption here, colonised.

So the true relationship is plausibly biphasic: too little NO impairs healing, too much sustains inflammatory damage, and the therapeutic goal is to move a chronically elevated wound back toward an optimum rather than to drive iNOS output to zero. Our coupling encodes only the descending limb. Within the range a competitive antagonist can reach — partial, reversible blockade of one receptor, against a wound already far above baseline — we think the monotonic approximation is defensible. But it is an approximation, it is not conservative, and a dose–response for healing outcome rather than for nitrite is the experiment that would settle it. We would look for an optimum, not a plateau.

Model 6

Wound closure under continuous and time-limited treatment

1. Background and evidence status

Model 6 uses the Fisher–KPP reaction–diffusion equation to represent the advancing wound front. It is the least supported part of the chain: its rates are Tier-4 phenomenological parameters, and no planned experiment currently constrains them. Its outputs should therefore be read as testable model predictions rather than quantitative clinical forecasts. This evidence limitation is separate from the support for Models 1–2.

What this model calls “closure” is not re-epithelialisation

A key biological limitation is that Model 6 describes a fibroblast population repopulating the defect. In real wound healing, fibroblasts build the granulation tissue that fills the wound, but the epithelial surface is closed by keratinocytes migrating in from the wound margins and from hair follicle remnants — a cell type this model does not contain at all.

A deep wound must be filled with granulation tissue before epithelium can cross it, so the two are ordered and fibroblast repopulation is a genuine prerequisite. But they are not the same event, and the one we predict is the earlier one. Every closure time on this page should be read as a granulation-filling time, and as a lower bound on the time to a closed epithelium. Reporting the two separately — and adding a keratinocyte layer before claiming closure times — would be a major structural improvement to this model.

2. Model Construction: Fisher–KPP with drug-dependent proliferation and death

Wound closure is represented as a front advancing into unoccupied tissue as cells migrate and divide. The Fisher–KPP reaction–diffusion equation captures these processes through a diffusion term for migration and a logistic term for proliferation, bounded by a carrying capacity. This bound represents contact inhibition and finite tissue density. Radial geometry is used because the wound is approximated as a circular defect; it also allows the model to calculate the area-based closure fraction.

The numerical Fisher–KPP check is shown in Figure 2(b); it verifies the solver against the analytic front speed. The model adds a death term, d(Ie), to the standard Fisher–KPP equation. This makes the effective growth rate reff = rp − d, which can be negative. When reff < 0, the model predicts regression rather than slow closure. This feature allows the model to represent chronic wounds that do not close under some conditions; 18.7 % of the sampled parameter sets produced no closure. The same formulation also produced the predicted relapse after treatment stops.

An agent-based model was not used because its additional rules for adhesion, contact inhibition, chemotaxis and cell division are not constrained by the planned experiments. The continuum model uses fewer parameters, although two front-behaviour parameters remain degenerate. This limitation is reported explicitly rather than introducing additional unconstrained detail.

The same reasoning sets the limits of what we claim. This is a minimal model of one cell population. It has no keratinocytes, no myofibroblast contraction and no angiogenesis, and each of those omissions is recorded above rather than left for a reader to discover.

The model treats the wound as a fibroblast population spreading into a defect with logistic growth and a death term, giving a travelling front. In radial coordinates:

3. Simulated Results: relapse, not stalling

When the drug effect fades and reff turns negative again, the steady state behind the front, nss = K(1 − d/rp), falls to zero. The repopulated tissue then dies back and the front recedes past its starting position. We had assumed a sub-threshold pulse would simply fail to finish. It reverses. A 5-day or 10-day pulse produces temporary apparent healing followed by complete relapse, while 14 days closes a 2 mm wound. The minimum pulse duration for permanent closure grows roughly linearly with wound radius, from about 7 days at 1 mm to 34 days at 5 mm.

This resembles the relapse clinically observed in chronic wounds, and it is a falsifiable prediction rather than a retrofit — the model produced it before we looked for it. It is also the clearest argument for why treatment duration, not peak dose, is the variable that matters for this device. If the mechanism is real, under-treating is actively harmful rather than merely insufficient, because it costs the patient weeks and returns the wound to a worse position than it started from.

Inverting the chain: what the device has to do

1. Background and design question

Model 7 runs the chain backwards, from a wound to a device specification. One patch keeps tissue above the 80.9 µM V14 target for 11.8 h (7.7–15.5 h across the ranges of sink depth and diffusivity). No wound in the 1–5 mm range closes on a single patch: the continuous cover needed is about 18–95× what one patch supplies. But the patch need only be changed every 36 h — so for the 2.5 mm gel as built, a once-daily dressing change is enough. That is the single biggest change from the earlier 0.5 mm design, which demanded a change every 6.8 h, four times a day. A daily change gives about 49 % duty against a 33 % threshold, a relative margin of about 49 %. That threshold is computed from Tier-4 closure parameters.

The loading requirement follows straight from the transport model: to hold a target concentration at depth δ in tissue, the gel must start with enough that the released fraction, diluted into the tissue pore volume, still clears it.

The model reports a maximum change interval as its output. This gives a practical device specification that can be related to patch performance, while the treatment duration still depends on the wound.

How retention affects the redesigned patch

We began by writing retention up as something to be maximised. Running the coupled solve showed otherwise. Retention works by binding, so the free concentration that drives delivery is Ctotal/RV: residence time is gained and driving concentration is lost in equal proportion. Whether that trade pays depends entirely on how much headroom the peak tissue concentration has above the target.

The redesign changed this conclusion. At the old 0.5 mm / 738 µM design the peak was only 1.9× the target, so retention now lengthens the window. At 2.5 mm and 3 mg mL−1 the peak is 522 µM, about 6.5× the target, and coverage rises from 11.8 h at RV = 1 to 14.0 h at RV = 1.5, 15.5 h at RV = 2, and 16.7 h at RV = 3. The revised target makes moderate retention more useful; the partitioning still needs to be measured before choosing a retained formulation.

The solubility limit that used to sit behind this has moved out of the way entirely. A 80.9 µM target at 500 µm depth needs a total loading of only 26 µM (35 µg mL−1) at frel = 0.5, which is 1.2 % of the 3 mg mL−1 the team has now dissolved. Retention would only become unformulable above RV ≈ 84.6.

And for V14 specifically, the chemistry is not available. Neither heparin nor alginate-sulfate can help: both are strongly anionic, and V14 carries a net charge of −2 with no Lys, Arg or His, so they repel. Retaining V14 in alginate would require covalent tethering, which means the two arms of the patch would need different chemistry. We abandoned retention as a design direction on these three grounds together.

The device specification

Combining the delivery and closure models gives an end-to-end device specification. Each patch's 11.8 h of cover is passed through the 18 h drug-effect delay, which sets a maximum change interval of 36 h — and that interval is a property of the patch, not the wound. Wound size changes only how long treatment must continue, not how often the dressing is changed. Repeat dosing is therefore part of the design, not a fallback for a patch that failed to release slowly.

The predicted 36 h interval follows from the model's average drug effect over a cycle. It equals the fraction of time a patch keeps tissue above target. Above 2.3 % the wound stops getting worse. To reach the density we count as closed, the average must exceed 33 %, so 11.8 h of cover sustains closure for change intervals up to about 36 h. A once-daily change needs 7.9 h of cover per patch, and the 2.5 mm patch supplies 11.8 h — a duty cycle of 49 % against a 33 % requirement.

This margin is a model prediction. The ratio of 11.8 h coverage to the approximately 7.9 h daily requirement gives about a 49 % relative margin. A roughly 33 % coverage shortfall would exhaust it; a 15 % shortfall would still leave about 10 h of cover. The lower end of the 7.7–15.5 h tissue uncertainty band is below the daily requirement. The 33 % duty threshold itself comes entirely from Tier-4 closure parameters with no planned experiment. The model therefore supports once-daily dosing as a prediction, not as a demonstrated outcome. Further design reductions should wait for measurements of the closure parameters.

Gel thickness and dermal drainage, rather than gel chemistry, set the single-patch coverage window in this model. Thickness is the strongest design lever: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.5 mm gels give 5.0, 7.6, 9.4 and 11.8 h above 80.9 µM at the current loading. At fixed area and loading, increasing thickness from 0.5 to 2.5 mm makes the reservoir 5× larger and the pure-diffusion release timescale 25× longer, but extends coverage by only about 2.3×. A thicker gel holds the peptide longer while degradation continues inside the gel. A 4 mm gel gives about 13.7 h. By contrast, reducing kprot thirtyfold lengthens the window to 13.0 h, and moderate retention adds several hours — within the 7.7–15.5 h spread the tissue parameters produce.

Finally, FGF2-G3 is not the constraint. Even without any retention the 2.5 mm gel keeps it above its EC50 for 5.9 days, about five times the change interval. The closure model requires both arms to be satisfied at once, but the FGF2-G3 condition is met continuously after the first few minutes and does not constrain the schedule. Retaining FGF2-G3 for weeks is therefore not expected to improve the current design. V14 sets the schedule.

Retention enters as a retardation factor multiplying the time derivative. For a saturable binder such as heparin-functionalised alginate it depends on concentration; for simple partitioning it is a constant:

The constraint that kills it · free concentration falls as 1/RV
C_{\mathrm{free},0}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{tot},0}}{R_V}\le\frac{C_{\mathrm{sol}}}{R_V}

The last line sets the retention constraint for this device. This build sets RV = 1 and uses none of these. The gel is plain calcium alginate, with no heparin and no sulfation. The equations are recorded because they define the counterfactual this section analyses, and because a future build using covalent tethering would need them.

5. Sensitivity Analysis

To find which parameters matter most, we ran Morris screening followed by Sobol indices over the full joint prior. We expected kprot,V to dominate delivered dose and Dn to dominate closure time. The computed indices do not support either expectation. The largest total-order indices for delivered dose belong to KD,P (ST = 0.63), the loading CV,0 (0.50) and kprot,V (0.42); closure time by r0 (0.63), with Dn the least influential of the ten factors (ST = 0.014), because tc ∝ Dn−1/2 is a weak dependence and physiology bounds Dn to about 1.5 decades.

An earlier version of this page ranked kprot,V fifth at ST = 0.023. That low sensitivity was an artefact of our own code: the analytic surrogate for delivered dose carried a spurious extra factor Λ/δ in the depth average, which cancelled most of the kprot,V dependence. The depth average of C0e−x/Λ over [0, δ] is C̄ = M(1 − e−δ/Λ)/(εtδ), which is what the module's own documentation stated and what it now computes. Correcting it raises kprot,V to third place. Proteolysis is therefore an important uncertainty, but KD,P and loading remain more influential under these priors. The variance ranking does not establish which parameter is easiest or most useful to change in the device.

Two points affect interpretation. A Sobol ranking reflects the width of the priors as much as the physics, and KD,P and CV,0 have the widest ranges. And share of variance is not the same as what we can change: gel thickness Lgel ranks fourth for delivered dose but is a design variable we control directly, while the gel material — porosity, mesh size — contributes essentially nothing. The practical lever and the statistical leader are not the same parameter. Lgel illustrates the first caveat too: its share more than doubled (ST 0.13 → 0.29) when we widened its prior from 0.3–1 mm to 0.5–4 mm to cover the 2.5 mm build. That is a change of prior, not of physics.

Propagating the full joint prior through to closure gives a result we think is the most important single number in the uncertainty analysis: under the current priors, 18.7 % of sampled wounds never close at all. This is a statement about the priors rather than a prediction for any particular wound, but it is the main reason we keep the death term in Model 6. A model without it would return a finite closure time for every one of those samples, and would quietly convert a chronic wound into a healing one by construction.

One further result constrains how this page should be read. A one-parameter-at-a-time sweep — what any slider performs — captures only the first-order variance: 34 % for delivered dose and 51 % for closure time. Most of the behaviour of the delivery arm, and half of the closure arm, lives in interactions that no single-slider exploration can show. That is why this page reports the variance-based analysis rather than inviting readers to tune one parameter at a time.

NO-responsive sensing, and why it was eliminated

1. Background

V2 of the device was a closed-loop system. A living bacterial chassis embedded in the patch would sense wound nitric oxide (NO) through a NorR/PnorV promoter and respond by secreting the therapeutic payload. The attraction was self-regulation: the device would dose in proportion to inflammation and switch itself off as the wound resolved.

The promoter-activity experiment failed, and the sensing branch has been removed from the device. We retain the modelling here because it explains the failure quantitatively, and because it establishes a design constraint that any future closed-loop build must satisfy.

The model follows the signal along its whole path: NO generation by iNOS in infiltrating M1 macrophages, diffusion through the hydrogel while the molecule is consumed, NorR-mediated promoter induction at the chassis, and peptide secretion. The conclusion is that the architecture was infeasible on geometric grounds before any biology was attempted. Across the current 2.5 mm patch, the fraction of wound NO reaching the chassis is at best 2×10−12, and under central parameters about 10−33, far below any plausible NorR activation threshold.

This is a negative result and we report it as one. The argument below is what justifies the pivot to the open-loop, dual-payload design.

2.2 Governing equations

The signal must survive three stages: production in the wound bed, diffusion through the hydrogel while decaying, and induction of the genetic circuit at the chassis. We take each in turn, then derive the attenuation law that decides the module's fate.

Wound-bed NO generation. iNOS expression is switched on by NF-κB p65, so the per-cell production rate follows a Hill dose–response:

(1) iNOS induction
q_{\mathrm{iNOS}}\bigl([p65]_{\mathrm{nuc}}\bigr)=q_{\max}\,\frac{[p65]_{\mathrm{nuc}}^{\,m}}{K_{\mathrm{iNOS}}^{\,m}+[p65]_{\mathrm{nuc}}^{\,m}}

Where: q_{\mathrm{iNOS}} is the per-cell NO production rate, q_{\max} its saturated ceiling, [p65]_{\mathrm{nuc}} the nuclear p65 concentration, K_{\mathrm{iNOS}} the p65 level giving half-maximal induction, and m the Hill coefficient.

Macrophages are counted as an areal density N_{\mathrm{mac}} over a band of depth \ell_{\mathrm{mac}}, so the volumetric source is N_{\mathrm{mac}}/\ell_{\mathrm{mac}} times the single-cell rate. Subtracting the two routes by which NO leaves the bed, chemical scavenging and uptake into the patch, gives the mass balance:

(2) Wound-bed mass balance
\frac{\mathrm d[NO]_{\mathrm{wound}}}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{mac}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{mac}}}\,q_{\mathrm{iNOS}}-k_{\mathrm{scav}}[NO]_{\mathrm{wound}}-\frac{J_{\mathrm{patch}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{mac}}}

Every term is a volumetric rate. The areal density N_{\mathrm{mac}} must be divided by the band depth \ell_{\mathrm{mac}} for the source term to carry the same units as the sinks; J_{\mathrm{patch}} is the NO flux into the patch and k_{\mathrm{scav}} the first-order scavenging constant. Note that N_{\mathrm{mac}} is tabulated in cells mm−2 while \ell_{\mathrm{mac}} is in cm, so a factor of 100 is required when evaluating Eq. (2) in the project's CGS units.

NO chemistry relaxes in seconds, while macrophage infiltration evolves over hours, so the concentration tracks its production rate almost instantly. Setting the left-hand side to zero and neglecting the patch flux gives the quasi-steady form the register uses:

(3) Quasi-steady wound NO
[NO]_{\mathrm{wound}}\approx\frac{N_{\mathrm{mac}}\,q_{\mathrm{iNOS}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{mac}}\,k_{\mathrm{scav}}}

Evaluated on the culture-well basis (k_{\mathrm{scav}} = 0.01 s−1, macrophages only) this returns 36 nM, which is the figure \ell_{\mathrm{mac}} was calibrated to reproduce. The in vivo wound value is higher, about 3.18 µM; the full model attributes the gap to neutrophil co-production and to tissue rather than medium scavenging. Both figures are used below, and neither changes the conclusion.

Transport through the hydrogel. NO entering the patch must diffuse outward while being consumed:

(4) Diffusion with first-order consumption
\frac{\partial[NO]}{\partial t}=D_{\mathrm{eff,NO}}\frac{\partial^2[NO]}{\partial x^2}-k_{\mathrm{decay,NO}}[NO]

where x is distance from the wound face, D_{\mathrm{eff,NO}} the effective diffusivity in the gel and k_{\mathrm{decay,NO}} the first-order consumption constant.

The gel is not pure water: solute molecules must detour around polymer chains and are sterically obstructed by them. We use the same effective-diffusivity convention as the delivery models, so that one operator serves all three solutes:

(5)–(6) Effective diffusivity and Amsden hindrance
D_{\mathrm{eff,NO}}=D_{0,\mathrm{NO}}\,\frac{H}{\tau},\qquad H=\exp\!\left[-\pi\left(\frac{a+a_f}{\xi+2a_f}\right)^{\!2}\right]

Equation (6) is the Amsden obstruction-scaling hindrance factor, with a the solute hydrodynamic radius, a_f ≈ 0.6 nm the alginate chain radius and ξ = 14 nm the mesh size. The tortuosity follows the Bruggeman closure \tau=\varepsilon^{-1/2}. Note that the porosity does not multiply the result a second time; it cancels out of the transient term under the declared concentration basis.

For NO, a ≈ 0.15 nm gives H = 0.992, against H = 0.971 for V14 (a = 0.87 nm) and H = 0.889 for FGF2-G3 (a = 2.34 nm). Hindrance is therefore real but negligible for a molecule this small, and the tables below are evaluated at H ≈ 1; carrying the exact value shifts every penetration depth down by 0.4%, which is within rounding. The grouped transport variable swept in the analysis is 1/τ = 0.3–1.0.

Steady-state profile and penetration depth. At steady state Eq. (4) reduces to an ordinary differential equation:

(7) Steady state
D_{\mathrm{eff,NO}}\frac{\mathrm d^2[NO]}{\mathrm dx^2}-k_{\mathrm{decay,NO}}[NO]=0

This is solved by [NO]=e^{rx}, giving r=\pm\lambda and the penetration depth L_d:

(8) Penetration depth
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{k_{\mathrm{decay,NO}}}{D_{\mathrm{eff,NO}}}},\qquad L_d\equiv\lambda^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{D_{\mathrm{eff,NO}}}{k_{\mathrm{decay,NO}}}}

Two boundary conditions fix the constants: the wound face is held at the wound-bed concentration, and the sealed backing permits no flux. The profile is then

(9) Profile across the patch
[NO](x)=[NO]_{\mathrm{wound}}\,\frac{\cosh\lambda(L-x)}{\cosh\lambda L}

and the chassis, sitting at the sealed face x = L, receives

(10) Attenuation at the chassis
\frac{[NO]_{\mathrm{chassis}}}{[NO]_{\mathrm{wound}}}=\frac{1}{\cosh(L/L_d)}\approx 2\,e^{-L/L_d}

L_d is the distance over which NO largely self-destructs before diffusing further. For L ≫ L_d the exact sealed-face result is twice the simple exponential, so quoting exp(−L/L_d), as the main model document does, is the conservative choice by a factor of two. Every additional L_d of thickness costs another factor of e. The number that decides the module's fate is therefore not the promoter's sensitivity but the ratio L/L_d.

From signal to secretion. At the chassis, NO binds NorR, which activates PnorV as a Hill function of the local concentration:

(11) Promoter activation
\theta_{\mathrm{norV}}=\alpha_0+(1-\alpha_0)\,\frac{[NO]_{\mathrm{chassis}}^{\,n}}{K_A^{\,n}+[NO]_{\mathrm{chassis}}^{\,n}}

Induction then drives the standard expression cascade, with \theta_{\mathrm{norV}} the fraction of promoter capacity engaged, \alpha_0 the basal leakiness, K_A the NO level for half-maximal activation and n the cooperativity of the NorR hexamer:

(12) Expression and secretion
\frac{\mathrm dm}{\mathrm dt}=v_{\mathrm{tx}}\,\theta_{\mathrm{norV}}-k_{\deg,m}\,m,\qquad\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dt}=k_{\mathrm{tl}}\,m-k_{\deg,p}\,p,\qquad J_{\mathrm{pep}}=k_{\mathrm{sec}}\,p

Here m and p are intracellular mRNA and protein, v_{\mathrm{tx}} and k_{\mathrm{tl}} the transcription and translation rates, and J_{\mathrm{pep}} the secreted peptide flux. These equations survive the pivot (see Section 6).

3. Verification status

The problem solved in this section is analytic throughout. Equation (9) is a closed form, and the penetration depths in Table 3 are evaluated directly from Eq. (8) rather than from a discretised solve. There is accordingly no NO-specific numerical solver in the current codebase, and no convergence study is claimed for one.

What is verified is the operator this analysis bequeathed to V3. The same diffusion operator was carried into Model 1, where the numerical solver is checked against the Crank plane-sheet closed form (Crank, 1975, §4.3.2) under successive grid refinement (100 → 200 → 400 → 800 nodes). That test exercises the transport physics reused here; it does not, and does not claim to, verify a NO solver.

The analytic results above reproduce the two physical limits by inspection: attenuation tends to unity as the decay constant vanishes, and as the thickness goes to zero.

5. Literature verification

The model's central derived quantity, L_d, can be compared against independent measurements without any fitting. Direct measurements of NO transport in biological tissue report diffusion distances of 100–200 µm (Ledo et al., 2004). Our swept band, 11–93 µm, sits at or just below that range; the upper corner, 93 µm, is within 10% of the measured lower bound. The difference is expected: the measured figures are for tissue, while ours are for a hydrogel carrying the Bruggeman and hindrance corrections, and our sweep deliberately includes short lifetimes.

The conclusion is insensitive to this comparison. If we discard our own sweep entirely and adopt the most generous measured value, L_d = 200 µm, attenuation across the 2.5 mm patch is still 4×10⁻⁶, six orders of magnitude of loss and far below any usable signal. No value of L_d consistent with the measurement literature rescues the architecture.

The transport side is therefore anchored to experiment rather than assumed: free-solution diffusivity from Zacharia & Deen (2005), mesh size from Grassi et al. (2009), hindrance from the Amsden obstruction-scaling model (1998, 2001), and tissue lifetime from Thomas et al. (2001). On the genetic-circuit side, K_A = 50 nM comes from in vitro characterisation of the NorR–NO interaction (D'Autréaux et al., 2005), so the induction threshold is anchored to a published measurement rather than fitted here. It is not carried in the project register, and the main model document deliberately refers only to a likely half-maximal constant, so the 64-fold figure above should be read as indicative rather than exact. The geometric argument does not depend on it.

6. Model refinement and the pivot

The structural conflict. The device faced two mutually exclusive requirements. Managing wound exudate needs a dressing of at least about 1 mm (Mutya et al., 2019), and the payload-coverage analysis sets the V3 patch at 2.5 mm; NO sensing needs the chassis within roughly 0.19 mm of the wound bed. Because attenuation is exponential in L/L_d, this gap cannot be closed by tuning: it is a structural property of the geometry. We granted the sensor the most lenient physiologically plausible parameters throughout and it still failed by twelve orders of magnitude across the 2.5 mm patch, and by five even at the 1 mm minimum dressing thickness, where attenuation is 2×10⁻⁵.

A correction to the numbers, not to the conclusion. Earlier drafts of this section evaluated the table with t_{1/2} = 3–10 s and obtained L_d ≈ 65–220 µm. That is the medium-autoxidation half-life, not the tissue-consumption one, and using it here overstated the penetration depth by about 2.4×. Those drafts also grouped the pore correction as ε/τ rather than H/τ; the numerical range is the same, but the grouping now follows the declared concentration basis. The register separates the two NO rates. The shorter tissue lifetime makes the infeasibility argument stronger, not weaker, which is why the conclusion is unchanged.

The pivot. We restructured the device as an open-loop, dual-payload delivery system. The patch delivers V14 and FGF2-G3 on a fixed schedule, with dosing informed by the delivery and closure models rather than by a live sensor.

What was retained. The analysis was not wasted:

  • The two-layer transport operator. The bilayer reaction–diffusion problem with interfacial continuity and a zero-flux outer face. Its operator, solver and verification harness became Model 1 of V3. Only the initial condition, the species constants, the partition coefficient and the transient rather than steady solve differ.
  • The NorR/PnorV induction function. Retained intact as a Hill transfer function. One correction applies on reuse: V2's stated 10–90% sensing window is the window of the Hill term, not of the induction fraction, which carries the basal-leakiness offset.
  • The expression and secretion kinetics, Eq. (12). Relocated rather than discarded. They become an upstream production model for recombinant V14 and FGF2-G3 (expression titre, solubility, export or refolding, purification yield), with the induction fraction set to unity and the volumetric source reinterpreted as a batch titre. A units convention must be declared on reuse: the Avogadro factor in the lumped secretion rate is correct only if intracellular species are counted in molecules per cell. If they are expressed as concentrations, the conversion is multiplication by cell volume, not division by Avogadro's number, an error of order 1011 if transposed.

For any future closed-loop design, the binding constraint is L ≲ 2L_d. Satisfying it requires one of three changes: sensing at the wound interface rather than behind the dressing, a slower-decaying signal molecule, or a stable downstream marker that integrates the NO history instead of tracking it.

Model change: removing the NO sensor

The original design used a NorR/PnorV promoter to sense wound nitric oxide and trigger peptide secretion, forming a closed-loop device. The promoter-activity experiment did not produce a usable response. We therefore used the model to assess whether nitric oxide could reach the sensing layer at a concentration sufficient to activate the promoter.

For NO diffusing through a porous layer while being consumed, the steady penetration depth is Ld = √(Deff/kdecay). Using a half-life of 0.09–2 s and sweeping the tortuosity group over its plausible range gives Ld = 11–93 µm, everywhere in the swept range. The current patch is 2.5 mm thick, and the 1 mm minimum dressing thickness is the thinnest layer that would still manage exudate. Across the whole range, NO reaching the chassis is at most 2×10−12 of wound NO at 2.5 mm (2×10−5 at 1 mm) — far below any plausible NorR activation threshold. The full analysis is in Learning Cycle 1. The problem is geometric, not biological, and no promoter engineering could have fixed it.

The opposite bound leads to the same place. Measured in vivo wound NO (~3.18 µM) sits above the likely half-activation constant of the promoter, so had NO reached the chassis the promoter would have been saturated and permanently on. Either way the design had no usable dynamic range. We removed the sensing branch, but kept its equations: the two-layer transport operator became Model 1, and the expression and secretion kinetics became an upstream production model for recombinant V14 and FGF2-G3. The result is that NO is now an output of the model and never an input, and the device is open-loop and two-armed.

Dated corrections and refuted expectations

  1. 18 October 2026: daily-duty arithmetic and screening interpretation corrected. The current 11.8 h coverage per 24 h is about 49 % duty, not the previously quoted 36 %; the relative margin above the 33 % threshold is about 49 %, not 10 %. A roughly 33 % coverage shortfall exhausts that margin. The peptide section now distinguishes positive-gate evidence from unvalidated occupancy and mechanism assumptions, and its written gate matches the implemented cysteine-count and stability rules.
  2. 18 October 2026: captions and interpretation synchronized with current outputs. The peptide release half-time is 59 min and its serum-to-release half-life ratio is about 6.2. The transport caption now distinguishes surface coverage from depth-averaged coverage. The thickness sweep uses the current target and separates reservoir volume from diffusion timescale. The dose-requirement curves are labelled by absolute occupancy, and proteolysis is described as the third-ranked dose uncertainty. The screening figure and consecutive figure numbering have been restored; proposed scratch assays are identified as future work.
  3. “A once-daily dressing change is not within reach of thickness or loading alone.” We said that, and on 16 Oct 2026 we were proved wrong by our own wet lab. The claim was true of the patch it described — 0.5 mm of gel, 1 mg mL−1 of V14 — which held tissue above target for only 2.2 h and so needed changing every 6.8 h. The team then cast the gel at 2.5 mm and loaded 3 mg mL−1, and the same solver now returns 11.8 h of cover and a 36 h limit. Thickness was the lever, and we had under-rated it because we were reading its effect off a prior that only ran to 1 mm. Two caveats keep this honest: the released fraction is now taken as frel = 0.5 rather than 1, which costs half the loading back; and the daily relative margin is about 49 % on Tier-4 closure parameters, so this is a prediction, not a demonstration.
  4. “The gel Damköhler number is small, so nearly the whole dose escapes.” True at 0.5 mm (DaV = 0.007–0.07), false at 2.5 mm (0.18–1.8, and 0.57 at the register rate). In-gel degradation now eats a real share of the mobile dose. At fixed area and loading, increasing thickness from 0.5 to 2.5 mm gives a 5× reservoir and a 25× pure-diffusion timescale, while coverage rises from 5.0 to 11.8 h at the current target (about 2.3×). The analytic surrogate behind the delivery sensitivity analysis still assumes full escape and is correspondingly optimistic; every coverage number we quote comes from the two-domain solver, which does not.
  5. “A peptide with GRAVY = +1.16 may not dissolve at the required concentration.” Withdrawn on evidence. We flagged solubility as a possible showstopper against an assumed 1 mg mL−1 ceiling. The team has since dissolved 3 mg mL−1 in the casting buffer, and the thicker gel cut the requirement to 26 µM, so the demonstrated loading clears it roughly 47× over. The saturation solubility is still unmeasured — what changed is that it is no longer on the critical path.
  6. The planar closure estimate is a lower bound, not an upper bound. Curvature does accelerate the front, by 5–21 % relative to Cartesian — but the formula Rw/c neglects the time to form the travelling front, and that transient outweighs the curvature gain. Radial closure exceeds Rw/c by 4–15 % (found with the earlier parameter set; not recomputed for the register update).
  7. The far-field boundary condition fails exactly where the device works. A zero-flux deep boundary needs Ltis ≫ Λ, but Λ is 1.1 mm at k = 10−4 s−1 and 11 mm at 10−6, against a dermis of 1–2 mm. V3 therefore uses a Dirichlet systemic sink, with no distributed clearance term as well.
  8. The Jensen gap was quoted at 15 %; recomputed for this geometry it is 0.2–36 %, median 2.7 %. The inherited figure was neither typical nor an upper bound. The gap is small in most of the parameter space but reaches 36 % in the corner that matters most, and it is always in the optimistic direction.
  9. Retention is not a lever worth pulling — but our reason for saying so was overturned on 16 Oct 2026. We first wrote retention up as something to be maximised, then reported the opposite: at the 0.5 mm / 738 µM design the coupled solve showed it shortening the window (2.2 h at RV = 1, 1.5 h at RV = 2, zero by RV ≈ 3). At the 2.5 mm / 3 mg mL−1 build the peak tissue concentration is 3.6× the target rather than 1.9×, so the trade now pays and a shallow optimum reappears near RV ≈ 1.5–2 (8.7 → 9.5 h in the 16 Oct calculation). The conclusion survives; the argument for it does not. We still do not recommend retention, because the gain is under an hour, the same hour is cheaper to buy with thickness, and for V14 in alginate it would need new tethering chemistry.
  10. Changing the gel from GelMA to calcium alginate mattered less than its thickness. Alginate binds neither molecule and its 14 nm mesh barely slows them, so the gel material drops out of the sensitivity analysis. Thickness is what moved the answer, twice: it remains the strongest lever, and the move from 0.5 mm to 2.5 mm on 16 Oct 2026 took one patch's cover from 3.4 h to 11.8 h — enough to turn a four-times-daily dressing change into a once-daily one.
  11. A sub-threshold pulse causes relapse, not stalling. We had assumed a transient crossing of the rp > d threshold would simply fail to finish. It reverses.
  12. The iNOS rate was four orders of magnitude too high, and looked right because it was matched against nitrite data using a free-NO expression. Writing the two observables side by side is what exposed it.
  13. The dose inversion had the wrong scaling law, not the wrong prefactor. Factoring out KDapp is valid for occupancy and for Cheng–Prusoff but not for the inversion, turning a linear relation quadratic and over-estimating by up to ~2500×.
  14. The peptide screen's stability gate was compared against a stale release half-time — and has now swung back. Module C originally used 2–22 h, carried over from a thicker gel. Recomputed on the 0.5 mm patch, t1/2,rel was 2.3 minutes and the gate stopped discriminating at all: every candidate passed it, so the stated reason for rejecting TAP2 was half wrong. On the 2.5 mm patch as now built t1/2,rel is 59 minutes, and the gate discriminates again — it demands a serum half-life of about an hour, which TAP1, TAP2 and MD2-I fail. That moves the survivor set from TAP1, MD2-II and V14 to MD2-II and V14: TAP1 passes the structural gate outright and now fails on stability alone. No selection decision ever turned on this — V14 has the longest half-life of the set under either release time — but the gate has gone from vacuous to load-bearing.
  15. Our sensitivity code underestimated the importance of proteolysis. We previously reported kprot,V at ST = 0.023, fifth of eight. The analytic surrogate carried a spurious extra factor Λ/δ in the depth average, which cancelled most of the kprot,V dependence — and disagreed with the formula written in the module's own documentation. Corrected, kprot,V ranks third at ST = 0.42, while first-order variance for delivered dose falls from 52 % to 34 %. The expected dominance of proteolysis is still not supported: it ranks behind affinity and loading. This correction establishes greater sensitivity to proteolysis; practical control of the device is a separate question.
  16. “NO half-life” was one symbol doing two jobs. We carried 3–10 s in the written model while the constants register held 0.09–2 s, and neither was simply wrong: tissue consumption (which sets the penetration depth Ld) and autoxidation in culture medium (which sets free NO in a well) differ by about two decades. No single number is correct for both. They are now two named constants. Using the medium figure for tissue had overstated Ld by ~2.4×, which made the NO-sensing geometry look more viable than it was — the corrected 11–93 µm strengthens the learning-cycle conclusion rather than weakening it.

We also found one modelling error during solver verification, and we note it because it is easy to repeat. With retention, the loaded concentration has to be treated as the total (free + bound), so the free initial concentration is Ctotal/RV. Treating the loading as free puts RV times more drug into the model than the solubility limit allows, and over-predicts sustained release by orders of magnitude.

Proposed measurements mapped to model uncertainties

The model guides the next wet-lab measurements. Each recommendation identifies the quantity to measure, the parameter it constrains, and the downstream predictions it could change. These are recommendations rather than a confirmed experimental schedule. In particular, the scratch assay is proposed future work; no planned experiment currently calibrates Model 6. The list considers both expected information gain and practical readiness; it is not a numerical ranking.

  • □
    V14 stability in serum or with recombinant MMP-9 / neutrophil elastase, by HPLC. Fixes kprot,V, the highest-priority unmeasured parameter in the register (currently a serum half-life proxy of 6.1 h), and replaces the Cavaco estimate in the peptide screen with a measured kdeg. This measurement would improve the peptide screen and the transport model.
  • □
    Saturation solubility of V14 in the casting buffer. 3 mg mL−1 (2213 µM) is now known to dissolve, so this is a lower bound rather than the assumption it used to be. Measuring the true saturation point bounds how much further loading could be increased. It is not required to show that the current target is reachable, but would inform future formulation choices.
  • □
    Swelling and dissolution of the alginate gel in simulated wound fluid. Calcium alginate absorbs exudate and slowly dissolves as sodium replaces calcium. The model assumes a fixed 2.5 mm reservoir; swelling would dilute it and shorten the change interval. Weighing gels over 24 h is enough.
  • □
    Run the release assay against a RAW 264.7 layer rather than a stirred bath. Existing experiments probe individual modules. A combined assay could reveal errors at the gel–tissue interface or in the flux-matching condition that separate tests might miss. This measurement links Models 1, 3 and 5.
  • □
    The same release assay for FGF2-G3. Every FGF gel parameter is otherwise unconstrained, and it tests the predicted 2.6× arrival separation directly.
  • □
    Scratch assay on fibroblasts ± FGF2-G3, ± mitomycin C, reporting front width. One assay that supplies an otherwise entirely unsupported module and breaks the Dn / rp degeneracy. This is the only experiment that would move Model 6 off Tier 4.
  • □
    Two or three LPS concentrations in the existing flow-cytometry panel. Without them, the absolute KD,P cannot be recovered; only KDapp is identifiable. Also run a Schild analysis: a depressed maximum would invalidate the competitive mechanism the whole V14 arm assumes.
  • □
    Nos2 as one of the two pro-inflammatory qPCR markers. It is the only marker that parameterises an equation already in the model.
  • □
    Run the release assay to completion on a log grid from ~1 min. From the 2.5 mm gel, 95 % of the mobile dose takes 5.6 h (V14) and 14.8 h (FGF2-G3), so the assay must run for most of a day rather than an hour. It is now the highest-priority release experiment, because it measures both the timescale and the released fraction frel — currently a Tier-4 estimate of 0.5 for V14 and, for FGF2-G3, 0.45 ± 0.06 fitted to the updated BCA run at its noise floor. Every coverage number depends on it linearly.
  • □
    Test a patch-change protocol, not a single patch. The model predicts a maximum change interval of about 36 h for the 2.5 mm gel. A once-daily protocol is predicted to close, while a 36 h interval is at the modelled limit. This test would directly assess the predicted daily schedule, which has about a 49 % relative margin.

One limitation. If FGF2-G3 is assayed on fibroblasts and V14 on RAW 264.7 cells, the two arms are validated in different cell systems and cannot be validated together in vitro. A co-culture or sequential conditioned-medium experiment would be needed to test the combined effect directly.

Conclusion

Integrated results, evidence, and limits

The models informed two design changes. The peptide screen identified developability concerns with the iteration-1 candidate and informed the de novo design of V14. The nitric-oxide penetration calculation indicated that the original closed-loop sensor could not receive enough signal through the device layers, leading to an open-loop, two-payload patch. These conclusions came from model analysis alongside the experimental results.

What the chain says about the device as built is consistent across every model in it. The 2.5 mm alginate patch releases its mobile dose over hours and keeps tissue above the 80.9 µM V14 target for about 11.8 hours, while closing a wound takes days to weeks. A single patch therefore cannot close a wound, and repeat application remains necessary. The model predicts a maximum change interval of about 36 h, so a once-daily schedule falls within the predicted interval. This is less frequent than the four-times-daily schedule associated with the earlier 0.5 mm design. Thickness increased the predicted coverage, and the relative daily margin is about 49 % on Tier-4 closure parameters: predicted, not demonstrated.

Retention improves V14 coverage across the tested range: about 11.8 h at RV=1, 14.0 h at 1.5, 15.5 h at 2, and 16.7 h at 3. No optimum appears in that range; measuring V14 partitioning in alginate is needed before choosing a retention design. FGF2-G3 lasts about 5.9 days without retention, so V14 sets the schedule.

The model chain also has clear limits. Models 1–2 are predictive now. Models 3–5 are predictive in scaled form and wait only on calibration. Model 6 is a hypothesis built on Tier-4 parameters with no experiment behind it, and Model 7 inherits that uncertainty wherever it touches closure. The wet-lab experiments listed above are ordered by how much each could narrow these evidence gaps, with practical readiness also considered.
