Overview

Background and structure of the model chain

Biological background and modelling question

The modelling is organised as a chain. A screening model comes first and asks whether the peptide is suitable as a payload. Seven linked models then follow the amount of drug loaded into the hydrogel and carry it to a healing outcome, along two arms — a V14 arm (release, transport, receptor binding, signalling, nitric oxide) and an FGF2-G3 arm (release, transport, fibroblast growth, closure). The two arms meet at a single point, where the therapeutic state of the tissue sets the closure rate. Without that meeting point this would be two unrelated models rather than a model of a combination patch.

The biological context is important because each model describes one part of the wound-healing process.

Why chronic wounds can remain inflamed

Acute wounds pass through haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. Chronic wounds — diabetic foot ulcers, venous leg ulcers, pressure ulcers — stall between the second and the third, held there by a loop that sustains itself:

  • The receptor stays on. Bacterial LPS from a colonised wound bed, together with host damage-associated molecular patterns from dying tissue, engages TLR4. TLR4 cannot bind LPS by itself; it needs the accessory protein MD2, which holds the lipid A moiety and drives receptor dimerisation. In human diabetic wounds TLR4 expression and downstream signalling are elevated rather than transient (Dasu & Martin, 2014).
  • The signal drives a destructive program. TLR4/MD2 dimerisation recruits MyD88 and activates NF-κB, raising pro-inflammatory cytokines, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and matrix metalloproteinases.
  • Those products prevent repair. Chronic wound fluid carries roughly sevenfold higher total MMP-9 than surrounding tissue, mostly activated (Moor et al., 2009), together with neutrophil elastase (Ai et al., 2004; Yager & Nwomeh, 1999). That protease load degrades the provisional matrix cells migrate across, and degrades growth factors directly (Yager et al., 1997).
  • Degraded matrix and dying cells release more DAMPs back onto TLR4. The loop closes.

This motivates a two-payload patch. Growth factor alone would be delivered into a wound environment containing proteases that can degrade it, a limitation reported for exogenous growth factors in chronic wounds (Yager et al., 1997). The design therefore combines V14, which is intended to reduce inflammatory signalling, with FGF2-G3, which supports fibroblast growth. Model 6 combines these two effects in the predicted closure response.

The models have different levels of evidence, so their conclusions should not be treated as equally certain. Models 1–2 can be used to make predictions now, because their parameters come from the V14 sequence, the patch geometry and the literature. Models 3–4 run in scaled form; the current exploratory model-based estimate of 5.62 µM gives a provisional axis scale but does not validate absolute affinity. Model 5 is theoretical and unvalidated; no Griess assay is planned. Model 6 is a structured hypothesis: its coupling functions are phenomenological and no planned experiment constrains them yet. Every parameter in the register carries a provenance tier, and where a value is Tier 3 or Tier 4 the tier, rather than a citation, is the statement of confidence.

The device

The patch is a single acellular calcium-alginate hydrogel layer, 2.5 mm thick: 1 % w/v sodium alginate, cross-linked with 100 mM calcium chloride, in HEPES-buffered saline at pH 7.4. Its outer face is sealed by an impermeable backing and its inner face sits on the wound bed. Two active molecules are mixed into the gel during casting: V14 (SVQELLELLAAGL, 1355.59 Da), variant 14 of our own de novo designs against the TLR4/MD2 complex, and FGF2-G3, an engineered, highly stable growth factor. The device contains no live bacteria.

A note on naming. V14 and αTI-14 are the same molecule: SVQELLELLAAGL. The peptide-screening document uses αTI-14 and the device models use V14. This page uses V14 throughout and gives the alias at first mention in each section.

Peptide suitability

Choosing a payload that survives the wound and the patch

Module 1: Peptide Suitability

1. Background

In synthetic biology, selecting a payload is often treated as a binding problem: find the sequence with the highest affinity and proceed. For a therapeutic peptide delivered via a hydrogel patch to a chronic wound, this is insufficient. The wound bed is a highly proteolytic environment, and the patch itself imposes structural constraints (Yager et al., 1997; Moor et al., 2009). A candidate that ignores these developability factors will fail, regardless of its theoretical target affinity. We observed this first-hand: our initial candidate, TAP2, failed to produce positive results in our wet-lab inflammation assays.

To understand why, and to avoid repeating the mistake, we developed a mathematical screening model to formalize the payload selection process. The model acts as an in silico filter, evaluating candidate sequences across three modules: binding suitability, structural burden, and proteolytic stability. By applying this screen, we identified the critical structural flaws in TAP2 that likely caused its experimental failure, and used the framework to guide the successful selection of our final de novo peptide, αTI-14.

2. Model Construction

2.1 Assumptions

AssumptionJustification
The serum stability model predicts wound stability.No validated wound-fluid model exists; the Cavaco regression is the only fully specified sequence-based half-life model (129 peptides; Cavaco et al., 2021). Wound fluid is more proteolytic than serum, with elevated elastase and MMP-9 and depleted protease inhibitors (Yager et al., 1997; Moor et al., 2009), so the serum half-life is treated as an upper bound. The verdict is therefore robust to uncertainty in the absolute half-life: because the delivery race is cleared by a factor of ~8 (pure-diffusion) or ~2.7 (degradation-inclusive), the gate would reverse only if wound-fluid proteolysis exceeded serum proteolysis by more than those margins.
Structural burden is captured by Cys and Trp counts.Cys drives oxidative disulfide shuffling, and Trp drives hydrophobic aggregation at high loading concentrations (Shi & McHugh, 2023). These represent the two most predictable sequence-level failure modes for a hydrogel payload.
The 100 µM benchmark is transferable.MD2-I blocks LPS binding at 0.1 mM, representing the published state of the art for TLR4/MD2 peptides (Slivka et al., 2009). While assay conditions vary, this provides a necessary reference point for judging our candidate's potency.

2.2 Governing Equations

The model evaluates three independent modules, each returning a sub-score s_i \in [0,1]. Because these represent independent ways a payload can fail, we combine them using a geometric mean. This ensures that a near-zero score in any single module (e.g., severe aggregation risk) drags the overall score down, rather than being averaged out by high scores elsewhere.

S = \left( \prod_{i \in \{A,B,C\}} s_i \right)^{1/3}

Where: S is the composite suitability score; s_A, s_B, s_C are the sub-scores for binding suitability, structural burden, and stability, respectively.

The verdict is not based on the composite score alone, but is gated by the two sequence-level modules:

\text{Verdict} = \begin{cases} \text{suitable}, & s_B \geq \frac{2}{3} \text{ and } s_C \geq \frac{1}{2} \\ \text{unsuitable}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

Where: s_B \geq 2/3 ensures the candidate has no more than one cysteine residue (no intramolecular disulfides can form). s_C \geq 1/2 ensures the peptide's degradation half-life is longer than the patch's release half-time.

Module A: Binding Suitability

The payload competes with LPS for the MD2 pocket. We model this as a competitive binding equilibrium. The fraction of MD2 occupied by the inhibitor (\phi_{\text{inhib}}) and by LPS (\phi_{\text{LPS}}) are:

\phi_{\text{inhib}} = \frac{[P]/K_{D,P}}{1 + [L]/K_{D,L} + [P]/K_{D,P}}, \quad \phi_{\text{LPS}} = \frac{[L]/K_{D,L}}{1 + [L]/K_{D,L} + [P]/K_{D,P}}

Where: [P] is the peptide concentration; [L] is the LPS concentration; K_{D,P} is the peptide-MD2 dissociation constant; K_{D,L} is the LPS-MD2 dissociation constant.

The predicted blockade fraction \chi_{\text{block}} is the ratio of inhibitor-bound to total bound receptor:

\chi_{\text{block}} = \frac{\phi_{\text{inhib}}}{\phi_{\text{inhib}} + \phi_{\text{LPS}}}

Where: \chi_{\text{block}} ranges from 0 (no receptor blockade) to 1 (complete blockade).

We convert this to a sub-score by comparing the candidate's half-maximal inhibitory concentration (C_{50}^{\text{cand}}) to the literature benchmark of 100 µM (Slivka et al., 2009):

s_A = \min\left(1, \frac{C_{\text{bench}}}{C_{50}^{\text{cand}}}\right)

Where: s_A is the binding sub-score, capped at 1; C_{\text{bench}} = 100\ \mu\text{M}, the blocking concentration of the MD2-I benchmark peptide; and C_{50}^{\text{cand}} is the half-maximal inhibitory concentration of the candidate.

Module B: Structural Burden

Two residue classes carry developability risk. Cysteine forms disulfides, creating folding heterogeneity. We apply a progressive penalty:

s_{B,\text{Cys}} = 1 - \frac{n_{\text{Cys}}}{n_{\text{Cys}} + 1}

Where: s_{B,\text{Cys}} drops from 1 (zero Cys) to 0.67 (one Cys) to 0.33 (two Cys); n_{\text{Cys}} is the number of cysteine residues.

Tryptophan drives aggregation. We apply a monotonic exponential penalty:

s_{B,\text{Trp}} = \exp(-0.5 \cdot n_{\text{Trp}})

Where: s_{B,\text{Trp}} is the aggregation penalty; n_{\text{Trp}} is the number of tryptophan residues; the decay rate 0.5 is a design choice, as there is no quantitative aggregation model in the literature.

The structural score is their product:

s_B = s_{B,\text{Cys}} \cdot s_{B,\text{Trp}}

Where: s_B is the combined structural burden sub-score.

Module C: Proteolytic Stability

We estimate the serum half-life t_{1/2}^{\text{serum}} using the Cavaco regression (Cavaco et al., 2021) and compare it to the release half-time t_{1/2,\text{rel}} of the hydrogel patch. The stability score is:

s_C = \frac{t_{1/2}^{\text{deg}}}{t_{1/2}^{\text{deg}} + t_{1/2,\text{rel}}}

Where: s_C is the stability sub-score; t_{1/2}^{\text{deg}} \approx t_{1/2}^{\text{serum}}, the predicted degradation half-life; and t_{1/2,\text{rel}} is the release half-time of the patch (calculated as 0.196 L^2 / D_{\text{eff}} from the companion transport model).

4. Experimental Validation

To validate the screening model, we compared its predictions against our wet-lab flow cytometry and RT-qPCR data.

Validating Module A (Binding). We assessed the binding suitability of our final candidate, αTI-14, using flow cytometry on PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages. Competitive blockade of LPS–TLR4/MD2 binding was confirmed at 25 µM, fourfold below the 100 µM literature benchmark used in the model (C_{\text{bench}}). This experimental result justifies the maximum binding sub-score (s_A = 1) assigned to αTI-14.

Validating Modules B and C (Structural and Stability Burden). The model's primary value was its ability to predict payload failure before synthesis. It flagged TAP2 as unsuitable due to its two cysteines and three tryptophans (Module B). Our experimental data corroborated this prediction. RT-qPCR analysis of pro-inflammatory markers showed that TAP2 failed to suppress LPS-induced inflammation across both the M0 and M1 macrophage phenotypes.

Where: Flow cytometry validates Module A by confirming that the candidate physically blocks the target interaction at the expected concentration. RT-qPCR validates Modules B and C by demonstrating that the structural liabilities predicted by the model (in TAP2) translate to a failure to suppress downstream inflammatory markers in a biological system. M0 and M1 refer to the macrophage phenotypes: M0 is the uncommitted state (assessed at 6 h), and M1 is the pro-inflammatory state (assessed at 24 h).

5. Sensitivity Analysis

Because this is a discrete sequence-screening model, sensitivity analysis evaluates whether the final verdicts are robust to the model's two primary design choices, rather than sweeping continuous physical parameters.

Trp decay constant (\lambda_{\text{Trp}}). The decay rate of 0.5 is an assumed parameter, as no quantitative aggregation model exists. A sensitivity sweep from 0.1 to 1.0 demonstrates that the gate verdicts are entirely insensitive to this constant. Because TAP2 contains three tryptophans and αTI-14 contains none, the structural margin is sufficiently wide that any reasonable exponential decay preserves the outcome.

Release half-time (t_{1/2,\text{rel}}). The release half-time is governed by the gel thickness, calculated via the companion transport model. While the stability gate becomes more stringent at thicker geometries (failing TAP1 and MD2-I at the 2.5 mm design), αTI-14 maintains a passing verdict across the entire biologically plausible thickness range (0.5 mm to 4 mm).

Binding benchmark (C_{\text{bench}}). The 100 µM benchmark sets the ceiling for the binding sub-score (s_A). Varying this benchmark from 50 µM to 200 µM does not alter the relative ranking of the reference set. Because αTI-14 demonstrates a measured 25 µM blocking concentration, it saturates the sub-score regardless of reasonable shifts in the literature benchmark.

Models 1 and 2

Release from the hydrogel and transport into tissue

1. Background

The patch releases its load as a single burst rather than a sustained supply. From the 2.5 mm alginate gel as built, V14 reaches 95 % release of its mobile dose in 5.6 hours and FGF2-G3 in 14.8 hours, while wound closure takes 7–55 days. The 2.6× difference between the two molecules comes only from their size, because plain alginate binds neither of them.

2. Model Construction: spatial diffusion across two domains

A compartmental ordinary differential equation could represent the gel and tissue as well-mixed regions, but the main output needed here is a spatial concentration gradient. Macrophages are concentrated near the wound surface, while clearance occurs deeper in the dermis. The duration for which tissue remains above target therefore depends on the concentration profile with depth. A well-mixed compartment model cannot resolve that profile or calculate the depth-dependent coverage window.

Each term in the partial differential equation represents a specified process. Transport is modelled as Fickian because the payloads are dilute and no concentration-dependent mobility has been established. The mesh correction H(a,ξ) uses Amsden obstruction scaling for solute movement through a fibrous polymer network. Degradation is first order, consistent with pseudo-first-order loss when the enzyme pool is in excess. FGF2-G3 also has a Michaelis–Menten uptake term to represent saturable receptor-mediated uptake; V14 has no corresponding uptake term.

The gel and tissue are represented as two coupled domains with an explicit interface. This preserves the partition at the gel–tissue boundary and allows each domain to have its own diffusivity and porosity. Flux matching conserves mass across the interface. The porosity convention used in that condition was corrected after an error was identified in an earlier version.

Reducing to one spatial dimension is justified by aspect ratio: the patch is centimetres across and 2.5 mm thick, so over the region that feeds a millimetre-scale wound the lateral gradients are negligible compared with the axial one. This approximation follows from the geometry and would not apply to a patch whose width is comparable to its thickness.

Release half-times are 59 minutes for V14 and 154 minutes for FGF2-G3. The gap between the release curves and the closure window is the central result of this section: the two timescales differ by roughly 10–230× for the as-built gel — narrower than the 300–2000× of the 0.5 mm build, but still one to two orders of magnitude. Three consequences follow.

First, the gel diffusivity cannot be identified from closure data and has to be fitted from the release assay. Second, the model predicts only a small closure benefit from an unmodified gel — this is a prediction of the model, not a failure of it. Third, if release kinetics are to matter for efficacy at all, retention would have to be added to the gel; Model 7 evaluates the effect of adding retention to the current design.

Our gel is plain alginate, which binds neither molecule: FGF-2 showed no measurable binding to unmodified alginate (Freeman et al., 2008), and V14 has no hydrophobic surface to stick to. Any retention analysis on this device is therefore a counterfactual, and we label it as one.

Because the release problem has a corner singularity at (x, t) = (0, 0), where loaded gel meets a perfect sink and ∂C/∂x diverges as t−1/2, Crank–Nicolson oscillates there and the solver uses Rannacher startup. This has a direct experimental consequence: integrating the release flux on a uniform time grid would over-count early release several-fold, so the release assay must be sampled on a log time grid. The 2.5 mm gel makes this less punishing than it was at 0.5 mm — the V14 release half-time is 59 minutes rather than 2.3 — but the assay must now run for most of a day to reach completion.

2.1 Governing Equations

The transport equations, and why porosity cancels

All concentrations in the model are interstitial, meaning mol per cm³ of pore fluid. For a porous medium with superficial flux N = −εDp∂xC, conservation gives ∂t(εC) = ∂x(εDp∂xC). For uniform porosity ε, it cancels and does not appear inside the PDE. Our earlier version applied it twice, once in the diffusivity and once in the concentration basis, which under-predicts spreading.

Effective diffusivity · Amsden obstruction scaling, Bruggeman tortuosity
D_{\mathrm{eff},i}=D_{0,i}\,\frac{H(a_i,\xi)}{\tau},\qquad H(a_i,\xi)=\exp\!\left[-\pi\left(\frac{a_i+a_f}{\xi+2a_f}\right)^{\!2}\right],\qquad\tau=\varepsilon^{-1/2}

Porosity enters in only two places: the flux matching at the interface, and the conversion of loaded mass to concentration. At ξ = 14 nm, the mesh size measured for 1 % calcium alginate (Grassi et al., 2009), this gives Deff = 3.48×10−6 (V14) and 1.33×10−6 cm²s−1 (FGF2-G3), using the correlation hydrodynamic radii of 0.87 and 2.34 nm with D0 = 3.6 and 1.5×10−6 cm²s−1, and an alginate porosity of 0.99. These are the values every figure on this page is plotted from. The register also tabulates an anhydrous-sphere alternative (radii 0.73 and 1.68 nm, Deff = 4.37 and 1.85×10−6), which would give 4.5 h to 95 % release rather than 5.6; those radii are lower bounds on the true hydrodynamic size, so we carry them as the fast edge of the sensitivity range and not as the headline. The gel barely slows either molecule: steric hindrance is mild for V14 and moderate for FGF2-G3, and across the whole 5–50 nm mesh range the effect on release is small. The mesh size is measured on 1 % calcium alginate (14 nm mechanically, 26 nm from release), not yet on our own gel.

4.1 Biological interpretation and model handoff

The two arms have different target cells in different places, and this is what sets the geometry of this section.

V14 targets macrophages, which concentrate in the inflamed superficial wound bed, close to the patch interface. That matters for how we report coverage. Concentration falls with depth, so a time-above-target computed from the depth-averaged concentration is a lower bound on what the macrophage population actually sees. We report the lower bound deliberately rather than quoting the more flattering interfacial value.

FGF2-G3 targets fibroblasts, which sit deeper, in the granulation tissue being built. This is why the model averages FGF2-G3 over an active band δ rather than taking its interfacial value — and it is why the two arms cannot be validated together in vitro without a co-culture, since one is assayed on macrophages and the other on fibroblasts.

The deep boundary is a sink because the dermis is vascularised. Drug reaching the dermal capillary bed is carried away systemically, which is why the model imposes a Dirichlet condition there rather than a reflecting one. That choice is biologically right in kind. Its depth is less certain: dermal anatomy places a vascular plexus well above the 1.5 mm boundary we use, so clearance plausibly begins shallower than the model assumes. If it does, residence time is over-estimated and the single-patch window is shorter than 11.8 h. We have not resolved this, and it is the assumption in this section most likely to be wrong in the optimistic direction.

Where the drug ends up: the 1.5 mm dermis drains the gel reservoir into the systemic sink within hours, whatever the proteolysis rate. Our macrophage target sits in a thin surface band where concentration is always above the depth average, so a time-above-target computed from the depth average is a lower bound — we report it that way deliberately rather than quoting the more flattering surface value.

1. Background

The V14 arm can be run before any binding affinity is measured. In the scaled variables ψ = CV/KD,P and a = [L]/KD,L, fourteen parameters reduce to four, and half-maximal suppression occurs at ψ50 = 1 + a exactly, with no fitted constant. The 17 Oct positive-gate result does not determine KD,P. For exploratory modeling, equating its gate-frequency ratio with relative LPS occupancy gives a exploratory model-based KD,P estimate of 5.62 µM and a model-derived target of 80.9 µM. This estimate depends on that assumption, so neither value is an experimentally determined affinity or target.

2. Model Construction: equilibrium occupancy

Model 3 uses equilibrium mass action and does not include explicit association and dissociation rate constants, kon and koff. This choice assumes a separation of timescales: peptide–receptor binding equilibrates within seconds to minutes, while the concentration delivered by Model 2 changes over hours. Under this assumption, receptor occupancy tracks concentration without a measurable delay, and explicit kinetic rates would add parameters without changing the current predictions. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) could test this assumption. If the measured dissociation half-life were comparable to the delivery timescale, the quasi-equilibrium model would need revision; slower dissociation could also extend peptide activity beyond the prediction.

The competitive form follows from the assumption that V14 and LPS bind the same MD2 pocket. It predicts a rightward shift in the dose–response curve without a reduced maximum. A non-competitive mechanism would instead reduce the maximum response. This distinction makes a Schild analysis a useful test of the proposed competitive mechanism.

Non-dimensionalisation allows the model to run without an absolute binding affinity. The chain contains fourteen parameters, including the currently unknown KD,P. Rescaling concentration as ψ = CV/KD,P and endotoxin as a = [L]/KD,L expresses the predictions in terms of dimensionless quantities. The dose–response shape, sensitivity structure and half-suppression point can then be calculated without specifying KD,P. Measuring KD,P would calibrate the concentration scale, but would not change these scaled predictions.

V14 (αTI-14) is a competitive antagonist: it and LPS contend for the same MD2 site, so each occupancy carries the other's concentration in its denominator:

2.1 Governing Equations

Competitive occupancy at MD2
\theta_{\mathrm{inhib}}=\frac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}\!\left(1+\dfrac{[L]}{K_{\mathrm{D},L}}\right)+C^{\mathrm t}_V}=\frac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm D}^{\mathrm{app}}+C^{\mathrm t}_V},\qquad\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}=\frac{[L]/K_{\mathrm{D},L}}{1+\dfrac{[L]}{K_{\mathrm{D},L}}+\dfrac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}}}

Where: CtV is interstitial V14 concentration; [L] is the LPS concentration; KD,P and KD,L are the dissociation constants of V14–MD2 and LPS–MD2; KDapp=KD,P(1+[L]/KD,L); and the two θ terms are the fractions of the MD2 pool occupied by inhibitor and LPS.

What the flow-cytometry assay reports is a normalised median fluorescence, and this is where the identifiability limit enters: the half-maximal point of that readout is KDapp, not KD,P. Ratios between variants are clean, because the endotoxin factor cancels:

The assay readout, and what it can identify
\mathrm{MFI}=\mathrm{MFI}_{\mathrm{bg}}+(\mathrm{MFI}_{\max}-\mathrm{MFI}_{\mathrm{bg}})\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}(C^{\mathrm t}_V)}{\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}(0)},\qquad \mathrm{IC}_{50}=K_{\mathrm D}^{\mathrm{app}},\qquad \frac{\mathrm{IC}_{50}^{(v)}}{\mathrm{IC}_{50}^{(w)}}=\frac{K_{\mathrm{D},P}^{(v)}}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}^{(w)}}

Rescaling by the two dissociation constants now removes them from the problem entirely. The whole competition collapses onto a single curve family indexed by the scaled endotoxin load a, which for our register value of 1 µM wound endotoxin is a = 15.4:

The scaled variables · fourteen parameters become four
\psi=\frac{C^{\mathrm t}_V}{K_{\mathrm{D},P}},\quad a=\frac{[L]}{K_{\mathrm{D},L}},\quad\gamma=\frac{[\mathrm{TLR4}]_{\mathrm{tot}}}{K_{\mathrm{NF}}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\theta_{\mathrm{inhib}}=\frac{\psi}{1+a+\psi},\quad\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}=\frac{a}{1+a+\psi}

The suppression ratio then has no free constant left in it, and setting it to one half gives the result this section exists for — a half-maximal dose with no fitted parameter anywhere in it:

Half-maximal suppression, exactly
\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}(\psi)}{\theta_{\mathrm{LPS}}(0)}=\frac{1+a}{1+a+\psi}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\psi_{50}=1+a\;}

Where: ψ=CtV/KD,P is dimensionless V14 concentration, a=[L]/KD,L is dimensionless endotoxin load, and γ=[TLR4]tot/KNF is the scaled receptor level. Thus ψ50=1+a is an exact scaled dose; under the exploratory model-based KD,P estimate, it corresponds to about 92 µM. The design target of 80.9 µM is a distinct quantity, KD,P(a−1), and depends on that assumption.

The biology this rests on is a single mechanistic claim: that V14 occupies the same MD2 pocket LPS does, and does nothing else. Competitive antagonism is what produces the rightward shift with an undepressed maximum, and it is what makes the required dose scale with endotoxin load. An allosteric or non-competitive inhibitor would change the functional form rather than a constant, and the exact ψ50 result would simply be false. We therefore treat this as a hypothesis to be refuted rather than a premise — which is why a Schild analysis is on the wet-lab list, and why we say explicitly that a depressed maximum would send us back to rebuild this model rather than refit it.

Because ψ50 is exact, it doubles as a closed-form check on any numerical implementation of Models 3–4, and we use it as one.

This matters for the project schedule more than it might appear. It means the V14 arm was not blocked on the binding assay: the dose–response shape, the required scaled dose, and the sensitivity structure were all available in advance, and the measurement when it came changed the axis label rather than the conclusions.

1. Background

Model 4 carries receptor blockade through to transcriptional output; Model 5 calculates free NO and accumulated nitrite from that input. 2. Model Construction: signalling and integrated readout

Models 4 and 5 use Hill functions to represent the signalling response without a full reaction network. A detailed NF-κB model could include IKK activation, IκBα degradation and resynthesis, and p65 nuclear shuttling. Such a model would introduce more than a dozen additional parameters, which the planned experiments do not constrain. The current models instead use a monotonic, saturating input–output relationship between receptor occupancy and transcriptional drive. A Hill function is a compact form for this relationship, and its exponent can in principle be estimated from dose–response data.

This simplification does not represent signalling dynamics. NF-κB can oscillate, so a pulsed input may produce a different transcriptional response from a steady input with the same average. The steady-state response should therefore be interpreted as an approximation.

Model 5 distinguishes two calculated quantities. Free NO approaches a quasi-steady level on the modelled timescale, while accumulated nitrite integrates the predicted production rate. The model therefore reports both the instantaneous free-NO concentration and the integral for nitrite. The earlier version matched a free-NO expression to nitrite values from literature, hiding a 104× error in the per-cell iNOS rate; the current formulation keeps those quantities separate. These remain theoretical outputs, not a fit to team measurements.

A reduced sigmoid could describe a dose–response if such data existed, but no Griess experiment is planned. The internal constants are not calibrated, and no fitted IC50NO or napp is reported.

The assumption we are least comfortable with

Model 6 reads nitrite as a monotonic index of harm: more nitrite means lower proliferation and higher death. The biology does not straightforwardly support that. Nitric oxide is required for normal repair — iNOS-deficient animals and animals given iNOS inhibitors show delayed re-epithelialisation and reduced collagen deposition and tensile strength, and NO has a central role in the angiogenesis that granulation tissue depends on (Krausz & Friedman, 2015). Macrophage-derived NO also contributes to antimicrobial defence in a wound that is, by assumption here, colonised.

So the true relationship is plausibly biphasic: too little NO impairs healing, too much sustains inflammatory damage, and the therapeutic goal is to move a chronically elevated wound back toward an optimum rather than to drive iNOS output to zero. Our coupling encodes only the descending limb. Within the range a competitive antagonist can reach — partial, reversible blockade of one receptor, against a wound already far above baseline — we think the monotonic approximation is defensible. But it is an approximation, it is not conservative, and a dose–response for healing outcome rather than for nitrite is the experiment that would settle it. We would look for an optimum, not a plateau.

5. Sensitivity Analysis

To find which parameters matter most, we ran Morris screening followed by Sobol indices over the full joint prior. We expected kprot,V to dominate delivered dose and Dn to dominate closure time. The computed indices confirm the first and refute the second. Delivered dose is dominated by KD,P (ST = 0.63), the loading CV,0 (0.50) and kprot,V (0.42); closure time by r0 (0.63), with Dn the least influential of the ten factors (ST = 0.014), because tc ∝ Dn−1/2 is a weak dependence and physiology bounds Dn to about 1.5 decades.

An earlier version of this page reported that both expectations had been refuted, with kprot,V ranking fifth at ST = 0.023. That was an artefact of our own code: the analytic surrogate for delivered dose carried a spurious extra factor Λ/δ in the depth average, which cancelled most of the kprot,V dependence. The depth average of C0e−x/Λ over [0, δ] is C̄ = M(1 − e−δ/Λ)/(εtδ), which is what the module's own documentation stated and what it now computes. Correcting it raises kprot,V to third place and removes a tension we had previously had to explain away: the design section argues that kprot,V is the most useful parameter to act on, and the variance ranking now agrees with that instead of contradicting it. We record the error because the corrected result is less flattering to our original framing, not more.

Two points affect interpretation. A Sobol ranking reflects the width of the priors as much as the physics, and KD,P and CV,0 have the widest ranges. And share of variance is not the same as what we can change: gel thickness Lgel ranks fourth for delivered dose but is a design variable we control directly, while the gel material — porosity, mesh size — contributes essentially nothing. The practical lever and the statistical leader are not the same parameter. Lgel illustrates the first caveat too: its share more than doubled (ST 0.13 → 0.29) when we widened its prior from 0.3–1 mm to 0.5–4 mm to cover the 2.5 mm build. That is a change of prior, not of physics.

Propagating the full joint prior through to closure gives a result we think is the most important single number in the uncertainty analysis: under the current priors, 18.7 % of sampled wounds never close at all. This is a statement about the priors rather than a prediction for any particular wound, but it is the main reason we keep the death term in Model 6. A model without it would return a finite closure time for every one of those samples, and would quietly convert a chronic wound into a healing one by construction.

One further result constrains how this page should be read. A one-parameter-at-a-time sweep — what any slider performs — captures only the first-order variance: 34 % for delivered dose and 51 % for closure time. Most of the behaviour of the delivery arm, and half of the closure arm, lives in interactions that no single-slider exploration can show. That is why this page reports the variance-based analysis rather than inviting readers to tune one parameter at a time.

Model 6

Wound closure under continuous and time-limited treatment

1. Background and evidence status

Model 6 uses the Fisher–KPP reaction–diffusion equation to represent the advancing wound front. It is the least supported part of the chain: its rates are Tier-4 phenomenological parameters, and no planned experiment currently constrains them. Its outputs should therefore be read as testable model predictions rather than quantitative clinical forecasts. This evidence limitation is separate from the support for Models 1–2.

What this model calls “closure” is not re-epithelialisation

A key biological limitation is that Model 6 describes a fibroblast population repopulating the defect. In real wound healing, fibroblasts build the granulation tissue that fills the wound, but the epithelial surface is closed by keratinocytes migrating in from the wound margins and from hair follicle remnants — a cell type this model does not contain at all.

A deep wound must be filled with granulation tissue before epithelium can cross it, so the two are ordered and fibroblast repopulation is a genuine prerequisite. But they are not the same event, and the one we predict is the earlier one. Every closure time on this page should be read as a granulation-filling time, and as a lower bound on the time to a closed epithelium. Reporting the two separately — and adding a keratinocyte layer before claiming closure times — would be a major structural improvement to this model.

2. Model Construction: Fisher–KPP with drug-dependent proliferation and death

Wound closure is represented as a front advancing into unoccupied tissue as cells migrate and divide. The Fisher–KPP reaction–diffusion equation captures these processes through a diffusion term for migration and a logistic term for proliferation, bounded by a carrying capacity. This bound represents contact inhibition and finite tissue density. Radial geometry is used because the wound is approximated as a circular defect; it also allows the model to calculate the area-based closure fraction.

The model adds a death term, d(Ie), to the standard Fisher–KPP equation. This makes the effective growth rate reff = rp − d, which can be negative. When reff < 0, the model predicts regression rather than slow closure. This feature allows the model to represent chronic wounds that do not close under some conditions; 18.7 % of the sampled parameter sets produced no closure. The same formulation also produced the predicted relapse after treatment stops.

An agent-based model was not used because its additional rules for adhesion, contact inhibition, chemotaxis and cell division are not constrained by the planned experiments. The continuum model uses fewer parameters, although two front-behaviour parameters remain degenerate. This limitation is reported explicitly rather than introducing additional unconstrained detail.

The same reasoning sets the limits of what we claim. This is a minimal model of one cell population. It has no keratinocytes, no myofibroblast contraction and no angiogenesis, and each of those omissions is recorded above rather than left for a reader to discover.

The model treats the wound as a fibroblast population spreading into a defect with logistic growth and a death term, giving a travelling front. In radial coordinates:

3. Simulated Results: relapse, not stalling

When the drug effect fades and reff turns negative again, the steady state behind the front, nss = K(1 − d/rp), falls to zero. The repopulated tissue then dies back and the front recedes past its starting position. We had assumed a sub-threshold pulse would simply fail to finish. It reverses. A 5-day or 10-day pulse produces temporary apparent healing followed by complete relapse, while 14 days closes a 2 mm wound. The minimum pulse duration for permanent closure grows roughly linearly with wound radius, from about 7 days at 1 mm to 34 days at 5 mm.

This resembles the relapse clinically observed in chronic wounds, and it is a falsifiable prediction rather than a retrofit — the model produced it before we looked for it. It is also the clearest argument for why treatment duration, not peak dose, is the variable that matters for this device. If the mechanism is real, under-treating is actively harmful rather than merely insufficient, because it costs the patient weeks and returns the wound to a worse position than it started from.

Model 7

Inverting the chain: what the device has to do

1. Background and design question

Model 7 runs the chain backwards, from a wound to a device specification. One patch keeps tissue above the 80.9 µM V14 target for 11.8 h (7.7–15.5 h across the ranges of sink depth and diffusivity). No wound in the 1–5 mm range closes on a single patch: the continuous cover needed is about 18–95× what one patch supplies. But the patch need only be changed every 36 h — so for the 2.5 mm gel as built, a once-daily dressing change is enough. That is the single biggest change from the earlier 0.5 mm design, which demanded a change every 6.8 h, four times a day. The margin is real but thin: a daily change runs at a 36 % duty cycle against a 33 % threshold, and that threshold is computed from Tier-4 closure parameters.

The loading requirement follows straight from the transport model: to hold a target concentration at depth δ in tissue, the gel must start with enough that the released fraction, diluted into the tissue pore volume, still clears it.

2. Model Construction: invert the chain to obtain a device specification

2.1 Governing Equations

Models 1–6 predict outcomes for a specified patch. Model 7 reverses this chain, starting from the desired tissue response to calculate a device specification. It relates gel thickness, peptide loading and dressing-change interval to the modelled treatment outcome.

The inversion requires careful algebra. The required dose is obtained by separating ψ additively; factoring out KDapp instead would turn a linear relation into a quadratic one. That earlier error affected the scaling law rather than a single constant, so it was identified through a direct numerical check.

The delay between drug arrival and tissue response is represented by an effect compartment. This standard pharmacodynamic approach approximates the combined delay from transcription, translation and matrix turnover with one parameter, ke0. That parameter is Tier 4 and remains unmeasured.

The model reports a maximum change interval as its output. This gives a practical device specification that can be related to patch performance, while the treatment duration still depends on the wound.

How retention affects the redesigned patch

We began by writing retention up as something to be maximised. Running the coupled solve showed otherwise. Retention works by binding, so the free concentration that drives delivery is Ctotal/RV: residence time is gained and driving concentration is lost in equal proportion. Whether that trade pays depends entirely on how much headroom the peak tissue concentration has above the target.

The redesign changed this conclusion. At the old 0.5 mm / 738 µM design the peak was only 1.9× the target, so retention now lengthens the window. At 2.5 mm and 3 mg mL−1 the peak is 522 µM, about 6.5× the target, and coverage rises from 11.8 h at RV = 1 to 14.0 h at RV = 1.5, 15.5 h at RV = 2, and 16.7 h at RV = 3. The revised target makes moderate retention more useful; the partitioning still needs to be measured before choosing a retained formulation.

The solubility limit that used to sit behind this has moved out of the way entirely. A 80.9 µM target at 500 µm depth needs a total loading of only 26 µM (35 µg mL−1) at frel = 0.5, which is 1.2 % of the 3 mg mL−1 the team has now dissolved. Retention would only become unformulable above RV ≈ 84.6.

And for V14 specifically, the chemistry is not available. Neither heparin nor alginate-sulfate can help: both are strongly anionic, and V14 carries a net charge of −2 with no Lys, Arg or His, so they repel. Retaining V14 in alginate would require covalent tethering, which means the two arms of the patch would need different chemistry. We abandoned retention as a design direction on these three grounds together.

The device specification

Combining the delivery and closure models gives an end-to-end device specification. Each patch's 11.8 h of cover is passed through the 18 h drug-effect delay, which sets a maximum change interval of 36 h — and that interval is a property of the patch, not the wound. Wound size changes only how long treatment must continue, not how often the dressing is changed. Repeat dosing is therefore part of the design, not a fallback for a patch that failed to release slowly.

The predicted 36 h interval follows from the model's average drug effect over a cycle. It equals the fraction of time a patch keeps tissue above target. Above 2.3 % the wound stops getting worse. To reach the density we count as closed, the average must exceed 33 %, so 11.8 h of cover sustains closure for change intervals up to about 36 h. A once-daily change needs 7.9 h of cover per patch, and the 2.5 mm patch supplies 11.8 h — a duty cycle of 36 % against a 33 % requirement.

This margin is small. It is about 10 %, and the 33 % threshold behind it is computed entirely from Tier-4 closure parameters with no planned experiment. A 15 % shortfall in coverage — well inside the 7.7–15.5 h band that the tissue parameters alone produce — would put a daily change back outside the envelope. The model therefore supports once-daily dosing as a prediction, not as a demonstrated outcome. Further design reductions should wait for measurements of the closure parameters.

Gel thickness and dermal drainage, rather than gel chemistry, set the single-patch coverage window in this model. Thickness is the strongest design lever: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.5 mm gels give 3.4, 5.5, 6.9 and 11.8 h above 80.9 µM. The gain is far less than proportional — a 25× larger reservoir buys about 4× the hours — because a thicker gel holds the peptide longer and the register degrades it inside the gel at the same rate as in serum. That a 4 mm gel gives about 13.7 h. By contrast, reducing kprot thirtyfold lengthens the window to 13.0 h, and moderate retention adds several hours — within the 7.7–15.5 h spread the tissue parameters produce.

Finally, FGF2-G3 is not the constraint. Even without any retention the 2.5 mm gel keeps it above its EC50 for 5.9 days, about five times the change interval. The closure model requires both arms to be satisfied at once, but the FGF2-G3 condition is met continuously after the first few minutes and does not constrain the schedule. Retaining FGF2-G3 for weeks is therefore not expected to improve the current design. V14 sets the schedule.

Model refinement: what changed and why

Re-running the model corrected several statements in the equation reference and did not support two earlier predictions. These revisions are recorded so that the reasoning behind each change is transparent and conclusions are not presented with more confidence than the evidence supports.

Model change: removing the NO sensor

The original design used a NorR/PnorV promoter to sense wound nitric oxide and trigger peptide secretion, forming a closed-loop device. The promoter-activity experiment did not produce a usable response. We therefore used the model to assess whether nitric oxide could reach the sensing layer at a concentration sufficient to activate the promoter.

For NO diffusing through a porous layer while being consumed, the steady penetration depth is Ld = √(Deff/kdecay). Using a half-life of 0.09–2 s and sweeping the tortuosity group over its plausible range gives Ld = 11–93 µm, everywhere in the swept range. The bacterial-cellulose and hydrogel stack of the earlier design was 0.5–3 mm thick. Across the whole range, NO reaching the chassis is at most 5×10−3 of wound NO even at the thinnest stack — far below any plausible NorR activation threshold. The problem is geometric, not biological, and no promoter engineering could have fixed it.

The opposite bound leads to the same place. Measured in vivo wound NO (~3.18 µM) sits above the likely half-activation constant of the promoter, so had NO reached the chassis the promoter would have been saturated and permanently on. Either way the design had no usable dynamic range. We removed the sensing branch, but kept its equations: the two-layer transport operator became Model 1, and the expression and secretion kinetics became an upstream production model for recombinant V14 and FGF2-G3. The result is that NO is now an output of the model and never an input, and the device is open-loop and two-armed.

  13. Our own sensitivity code had a bug, and it had reversed one of our conclusions. We reported that the expected dominance of kprot,V over delivered dose had been refuted (ST = 0.023, fifth of eight). The analytic surrogate carried a spurious extra factor Λ/δ in the depth average, which cancelled most of the kprot,V dependence — and disagreed with the formula written in the module's own documentation. Corrected, kprot,V ranks third at ST = 0.42 and the expectation is confirmed, while first-order variance for delivered dose falls from 52 % to 34 %. The correction also resolved a tension we had been explaining away: our design section calls kprot,V the most useful parameter to act on, and the variance ranking now agrees instead of contradicting it. The lesson we take is that a result which conveniently explained an anomaly deserved more suspicion than we gave it.
  14. “NO half-life” was one symbol doing two jobs. We carried 3–10 s in the written model while the constants register held 0.09–2 s, and neither was simply wrong: tissue consumption (which sets the penetration depth Ld) and autoxidation in culture medium (which sets free NO in a well) differ by about two decades. No single number is correct for both. They are now two named constants. Using the medium figure for tissue had overstated Ld by ~2.4×, which made the NO-sensing geometry look more viable than it was — the corrected 11–93 µm strengthens the learning-cycle conclusion rather than weakening it.

We also found one modelling error during solver verification, and we note it because it is easy to repeat. With retention, the loaded concentration has to be treated as the total (free + bound), so the free initial concentration is Ctotal/RV. Treating the loading as free puts RV times more drug into the model than the solubility limit allows, and over-predicts sustained release by orders of magnitude.

Experimental plan mapped to model uncertainties

The model guides the next wet-lab measurements. Each recommendation identifies the quantity to measure, the parameter it constrains, and the downstream predictions it could change. The list considers both expected information gain and practical readiness; it is not a numerical ranking.

  • □
    V14 stability in serum or with recombinant MMP-9 / neutrophil elastase, by HPLC. Fixes kprot,V, the highest-priority unmeasured parameter in the register (currently a serum half-life proxy of 6.1 h), and replaces the Cavaco estimate in the peptide screen with a measured kdeg. This measurement would improve the peptide screen and the transport model.
  • □
    Saturation solubility of V14 in the casting buffer. 3 mg mL−1 (2213 µM) is now known to dissolve, so this is a lower bound rather than the assumption it used to be. Measuring the true saturation point bounds how much further loading could be increased. It is not required to show that the current target is reachable, but would inform future formulation choices.
  • □
    Swelling and dissolution of the alginate gel in simulated wound fluid. Calcium alginate absorbs exudate and slowly dissolves as sodium replaces calcium. The model assumes a fixed 2.5 mm reservoir; swelling would dilute it and shorten the change interval. Weighing gels over 24 h is enough.
  • □
    Run the release assay against a RAW 264.7 layer rather than a stirred bath. Existing experiments probe individual modules. A combined assay could reveal errors at the gel–tissue interface or in the flux-matching condition that separate tests might miss. This measurement links Models 1, 3 and 5.
  • □
    The same release assay for FGF2-G3. Every FGF gel parameter is otherwise unconstrained, and it tests the predicted 2.6× arrival separation directly.
  • □
    Scratch assay on fibroblasts ± FGF2-G3, ± mitomycin C, reporting front width. One assay that supplies an otherwise entirely unsupported module and breaks the Dn / rp degeneracy. This is the only experiment that would move Model 6 off Tier 4.
  • □
    Two or three LPS concentrations in the existing flow-cytometry panel. Without them, the absolute KD,P cannot be recovered; only KDapp is identifiable. Also run a Schild analysis: a depressed maximum would invalidate the competitive mechanism the whole V14 arm assumes.
  • □
    Nos2 as one of the two pro-inflammatory qPCR markers. It is the only marker that parameterises an equation already in the model.
  • □
    Run the release assay to completion on a log grid from ~1 min. From the 2.5 mm gel, 95 % of the mobile dose takes 5.6 h (V14) and 14.8 h (FGF2-G3), so the assay must run for most of a day rather than an hour. It is now the highest-priority release experiment, because it measures both the timescale and the released fraction frel — currently a Tier-4 estimate of 0.5 for V14 and, for FGF2-G3, 0.45 ± 0.06 fitted to the updated BCA run at its noise floor. Every coverage number depends on it linearly.
  • □
    Test a patch-change protocol, not a single patch. The model predicts a maximum change interval of about 36 h for the 2.5 mm gel. A once-daily protocol is predicted to close, while a 36 h interval is at the modelled limit. This test would directly assess the predicted schedule, which has a 10 % margin.

One limitation. If FGF2-G3 is assayed on fibroblasts and V14 on RAW 264.7 cells, the two arms are validated in different cell systems and cannot be validated together in vitro. A co-culture or sequential conditioned-medium experiment would be needed to test the combined effect directly.

Conclusion

Integrated results, evidence, and limits

The models informed two design changes. The peptide screen identified developability concerns with the iteration-1 candidate and informed the de novo design of V14. The nitric-oxide penetration calculation indicated that the original closed-loop sensor could not receive enough signal through the device layers, leading to an open-loop, two-payload patch. These conclusions came from model analysis alongside the experimental results.

What the chain says about the device as built is consistent across every model in it. The 2.5 mm alginate patch releases its mobile dose over hours and keeps tissue above the 80.9 µM V14 target for about 11.8 hours, while closing a wound takes days to weeks. A single patch therefore cannot close a wound, and repeat application remains necessary. The model predicts a maximum change interval of about 36 h, so a once-daily schedule falls within the predicted interval. This is less frequent than the four-times-daily schedule associated with the earlier 0.5 mm design. Thickness increased the predicted coverage, and the margin is about 10 % on Tier-4 closure parameters: predicted, not demonstrated.

Retention improves V14 coverage across the tested range: about 11.8 h at RV=1, 14.0 h at 1.5, 15.5 h at 2, and 16.7 h at 3. No optimum appears in that range; measuring V14 partitioning in alginate is needed before choosing a retention design. FGF2-G3 lasts about 5.9 days without retention, so V14 sets the schedule.

The model chain also has clear limits. Models 1–2 are predictive now. Models 3–5 are predictive in scaled form and wait only on calibration. Model 6 is a hypothesis built on Tier-4 parameters with no experiment behind it, and Model 7 inherits that uncertainty wherever it touches closure. The wet-lab experiments listed above are ordered by how much each could narrow these evidence gaps, with practical readiness also considered.
